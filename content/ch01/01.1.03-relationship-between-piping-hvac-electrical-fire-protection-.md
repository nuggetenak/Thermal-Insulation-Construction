# Relationship between piping, HVAC, electrical, fire protection and insulation

## What it is

Insulation has an unusual relationship with the trades around it. Most trades
work beside each other. Insulation works **on top of** other people's finished
work, and then hides it.

That gives the job two properties nothing else on the floor has. Your work
cannot start until theirs is finished and proved. And once your work is on,
theirs cannot be seen, tested or corrected without taking yours off again.

Everything in this item follows from those two sentences.

## Why it matters on the job

Because the cost of getting the interface wrong is not a defect in your work. It
is the destruction of your work.

Insulate a pipe that has not been pressure tested, and when the test finds a
weeping joint, the insulation over that joint comes off, and possibly a long way
either side of it. Nothing you did was badly done. It was done at the wrong
time, and the material is now waste.

That is the failure mode a first-year helper is most likely to cause, because it
does not look like a mistake while it is being made. The pipe is there, the
material is on the van, the supervisor is on another floor, and starting looks
like initiative.

**Before starting on any run, the question is not whether the pipe is finished.
It is whether the system has been tested and released to your crew.**

## In detail

### Rule one: testing comes before insulation, always

This is not a site convention. It is written into the standard specification
that governs mechanical services work on Japanese government building projects,
and which most private specifications in Japan follow closely. That
specification requires the system tests to be carried out before any of three
things happens: before the work is concealed or backfilled, before the pipework
is painted, and before insulation is applied.

The tests themselves depend on the system. Water pipework, steam and high
temperature water, chilled water, brine and oil lines are proved by pressure
test — 水圧試験 for the water systems, air pressure for oil. Drainage is proved
by filling it. Refrigerant pipework is proved by 気密試験, and the same
specification states plainly that insulation on refrigerant lines is carried out
after that airtightness test is complete.

Two consequences for you.

**A tested system is a released system, and someone says so.** Testing is
witnessed and recorded. The release is a piece of information that has to travel
from the pipe installer to your supervisor to you. If it did not reach you,
it did not happen — ask. See
[01.6.02](../ch01/01.6.02-sequence-who-insulates-before-and-after-whom.md).

**Painting is in that same list, and the order between painting and insulation
is not.** The specification says testing comes before painting and testing comes
before insulation. It does not say which of those two comes first. In practice
paint goes on before the insulation covers it — a coat of primer is not much use
under a wrap nobody will remove — but that is trade reasoning, not a clause, and
painting for mechanical work is governed by a separate document this reference
has not consulted.

What follows for you is unchanged either way: arriving to find bare steel where
you expected a painted pipe is a reason to stop and ask, not a reason to start.

### Rule two: a fire compartment overrides your material choice

A 防火区画 is a wall or floor built to stop fire spreading. Pipes and ducts have
to pass through them, and every one of those 貫通部 is a hole in a fire barrier
that has to be made good.

The specification's rules here point at compartments defined by the Building
Standard Act rather than at anything you could identify by looking. Which walls
on your site are fire compartments is information you are given, not something
you work out.

Two rules from the same specification matter to an insulation worker.

Where an insulated pipe passes through one of these compartments, the insulation
at the penetration itself is rock wool. Not the material you are using along the
rest of the run — rock wool at that point, because the requirement there is fire
performance, not thermal performance.

**This is not a chilled-water rule.** The specification states it twice, once for
chilled water and chilled-and-heating water pipework, and again for water supply,
drainage and hot water supply pipework. Between them that is most of what this
trade insulates in a building. Assume it applies to your run until someone tells
you otherwise.

Separately from the insulation, where a non-combustible pipe passes through such
a compartment, the gap around it is filled with mortar or rock wool insulation.

Where the pipe itself is combustible, the treatment has to be one that satisfies
the Building Standard Act, which in practice means a tested and approved
proprietary system rather than anything improvised on site.

The practical point: **at a fire compartment, you do not choose the material,
and you do not improvise the detail.** If the drawing or the method statement
does not show the penetration detail, that is a question, not a gap for you to
fill. Chapter 19 covers penetrations in depth.

There is a second, less obvious version of this rule. Steam pipework and lines
like it are not insulated right up to the face of a wall or floor they pass
through — the same specification keeps insulation back from the face by a stated
distance. The reason is heat, not fire, but the pattern is the same: what
happens at a penetration is decided by the building, not by the pipe.

### Rule three: some metal must not be covered in metal

A 絶縁継手 — a dielectric joint or insulating flange — is fitted deliberately to
break the electrical path between two different metals so that one does not
corrode the other. It is a small fitting and it does not look like anything
special.

The specification prohibits metal jacketing around a dielectric joint,
insulating flanges included. Wrapping it in metal reconnects electrically the
two things the fitting exists to keep apart, and defeats it completely. The
corrosion that follows appears months or years later, somewhere else on the
system, and nothing about it points back at the jacketing.

This is the clearest example of a general truth about the electrical interface:
the mechanical installation contains electrical features that are invisible to
someone who does not know they exist. The other one you will meet is electric
trace heating — a heating cable run along the pipe under the insulation. It is
an electrical installation, it has to be tested, and insulating over it before
it is released means removing your work to test it.

Two smaller cases in the same family:

- Refrigerant pipework runs as a liquid line and a gas line. The specification states that the two are not to be wrapped together in one common wrap. Two lines at different temperatures inside one insulation envelope do not both perform. There is one exception written into the clause: pre-insulated copper tube, which arrives with its insulation already on it, is excluded. If the material in your hands is pre-insulated, the prohibition is not what governs — ask rather than assuming either way.
- The electrical connection between an indoor and an outdoor unit is sometimes taped along the refrigerant pipe. Where it is, the specification puts that after the pipe is insulated, not before. It is a different rule from the one above and it is easy to run the two together.
- Earthing and bonding conductors are sometimes clamped to pipework. A bonding clamp buried under insulation cannot be inspected. If one is in your way, that is an electrician's item to relocate.

### Rule four: fire protection and access are not yours to move

Sprinkler heads, fire dampers, alarm devices and valve sets all have clearances
that exist so the equipment works or so a person can reach it. Insulation and
jacketing are bulky, and a run that fits the drawing can still end up sitting
where a damper access panel needs to open.

The specification is explicit about the general principle for equipment you do
insulate: doors, 点検口 and access openings must still open and be usable, and
the insulation must not lose its effect around them. The same thinking applies
to everyone else's access.

So: if your work will block a sprinkler head's spray pattern, cover an access
panel, or bury a fire damper's operating mechanism, that is a clash to raise
before you build it, not after. See
[01.6.05](../ch01/01.6.05-raising-a-clash-rather-than-working-around-it.md).

### What is not settled here

Three honest gaps.

**Who does what at a penetration varies.** The hole, the sleeve, the fire seal,
the insulation through the opening and the plate covering it can be split
between two or three companies, and the split is decided per job. Ask on the
first penetration you meet on a new site rather than assuming the last site's
arrangement.

**Japanese standard practice for insulation work is governed by JIS A 9501,
which is a paid document.** It is described here rather than reproduced. Where a
specification cites a standard value, that value governs over anything in this
reference.

**The specification quoted throughout this item governs public building work.**
Private jobs usually follow it, and the employer these readers are joining works
on government and hospital buildings among others. But usually is not always,
and the specification for your job is the one that applies. Confirm with your
supervisor which document your site is working to.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting on a run because the pipe looks finished.** Finished and tested are different states, and only one of them releases the work to you. Ask; the question takes ten seconds.
- **Insulating over a joint temporarily so the run looks complete.** There is no temporary insulation. It gets forgotten, the ceiling closes, and the untested joint is now behind a permanent finish.
- **Carrying the run's material straight through a fire compartment.** The penetration is a different detail with a different material, and rock wool at that point is a fire requirement rather than a preference.
- **Jacketing a dielectric joint because it looked untidy bare.** The fitting exists to break an electrical path. Metal around it undoes that, and the damage shows up as corrosion far away and much later.
- **Insulating over electric trace heating before it has been tested.** The heating cable is an electrical installation. Covering it early means uncovering it again.
- **Wrapping refrigerant liquid and gas lines together.** They run at different temperatures. One common wrap is prohibited by the specification and does not work thermally either. Pre-insulated copper tube is the written exception, so check which you have rather than applying the rule blind.
- **Building over an access panel and planning to cut it out later.** Cut openings in finished jacketing are a snagging item every time. Find the panel before the material goes on.
- **Assuming the last site's trade split applies here.** Who seals a penetration, who insulates through it and who fits the cover plate is decided job by job.

# General construction workflow

## What it is

A construction job travels a long way before it reaches the person holding the
tool, and it changes form at every step. It starts as money and a decision by a
client (発注者). It becomes a contract. The contract becomes a programme with
dates on it. The dates become a plan for how the work will be built. The plan
becomes a spoken instruction at eight o'clock in the morning: insulate these
pipes on this floor today.

That last sentence is the only part of the chain most first-year helpers ever
see. This item is about the rest of it, because the rest of it is where the
pressure on your day comes from.

## Why it matters on the job

Almost nothing that goes wrong for a new worker in the first months is a
hand-skill problem. It is a workflow problem.

Turning up at a site where your paperwork was never submitted, so the gate will
not let you in. Insulating a pipe that has not been pressure tested yet, so it
has to come off again. Being on the wrong floor at eight o'clock because the
sequence changed and the change reached your supervisor but not you. Finishing a
run correctly and not telling anyone, so nobody photographs it before the
ceiling closes over it.

Each of those costs a day or more, and none of them is about how well you can
cut a pipe section. They are about understanding where you sit in a chain of
companies and documents that you cannot see from inside a ceiling void.

## In detail

### The chain of companies

Japanese construction work is layered. A client orders a building. One company,
the 元請, holds the contract for it. That company subcontracts specialist
packages to other companies (下請), and those companies sometimes subcontract
further. A first-tier subcontractor works directly for the prime contractor; a
second-tier subcontractor works for the first-tier one.

An insulation company of six people is normally somewhere in that lower part of
the chain — often under a mechanical services contractor rather than directly
under the prime contractor. Who exactly you sit under is not a detail you should
guess at, and there is a document on site that answers it. See
[01.1.04](../ch01/01.1.04-subcontractor-and-site-organization-basics.md).

One thing the chain is not allowed to be is empty. The 建設業法, the Act that
licenses construction companies, forbids a contractor from taking a job and
passing the whole of it straight to somebody else without genuinely managing it.
That ban on 一括下請負 is why every company in the chain above you has to be
doing something real — planning, programming, checking quality, running safety.
A company that is only collecting a margin and passing the work along is
breaking the Act, not operating a business model. See
[01.1.02](../ch01/01.1.02-mechanical-equipment-trades-overview.md) for what the
Act licenses your own company to do.

### The chain of documents

Instructions get narrower and more specific as they come down the chain. Each
step is a real document with a name, and knowing the names lets you ask a
sensible question instead of a vague one.

| Document | What it fixes | Who writes it |
|---|---|---|
| Contract | Scope, price, completion date | Client and prime contractor |
| Drawings and specification | What is to be built and to what standard | The designer |
| 工程表 | Which work happens on which dates | Prime contractor |
| 施工計画書 | How the works will be carried out and controlled | Prime contractor |
| 施工要領書 (method statement) | How one specific task is done on this site | The specialist contractor |
| Today's instruction | What you personally do today | Your supervisor |

When something on site does not make sense, the useful question is which of
those the answer lives in. A dimension question is a drawing question. Asking to
move a task to next week is a 工程表 question, and not yours to settle. Asking
how a particular detail is to be built is a method statement question. Chapter
04 covers reading them; see
[04.4.01](../ch04/04.4.01-item-1.md).

On public work there is one more person in the picture: the 監督職員, the
client's supervising officer, who checks that what is built matches what was
specified. That person is not your supervisor and does not give you instructions
directly. Instructions reach you through your own company.

### The shape of a working day

The chain above produces a day with a fairly standard shape on Japanese sites.

**朝礼.** A whole-site morning meeting before work starts. Announcements, the
day's work by each company, any area closures, and often exercises. It is where
you learn that the floor you were on yesterday is closed today.

**危険予知, usually shortened to KY.** A short discussion within your own crew
about what could hurt someone in today's specific task, before the task starts.
Not a general safety lecture — a named hazard in a named place.

**段取り.** Getting materials, access equipment and tools to where the work is.
On a multi-site building services job this can take a genuinely large share of
the morning, and it is real work rather than a delay.

**The work itself.**

**片付け.** Clearing up. Offcuts, packaging, banding ends, tools, access
equipment. This is not optional tidiness and it is not the last ten minutes of
goodwill — on a shared site, the state you leave an area in is the main thing
other companies notice about your company.

Somewhere in that day, someone from the prime contractor walks through your
area. That is not a coincidence and not a sign that anyone distrusts you:
under the 労働安全衛生規則, the prime contractor on a site with mixed companies
must patrol the workplace at least once every working day. Expect to be looked
at daily, and expect to be asked what you are doing.

### The working year

Construction in Japan was exempt from the statutory ceiling on overtime for
years longer than other industries. That exemption ended on 1 April 2024, and
the general limits now apply: a monthly and an annual ceiling on overtime, with
a higher annual figure available only under a special agreement, and disaster
recovery work still excepted from part of it. Alongside it, the department that
writes the specification for government building work has been pushing sites to
close for eight days in every four weeks — a genuine two-day weekend on average
— and monitors whether they actually do it.

That is a target being measured, not a description of the industry. It applies
to public work, and how far it reaches a small subcontractor on a private job is
not settled by any source consulted here.

Two things follow for a new worker. The first is that a six-day week is no
longer simply assumed, though it is still common. The second is that when your
own hours are recorded at the end of a long week, that record has a legal
purpose. Report your hours accurately.

### Where insulation sits in the workflow

Insulation is late work. The pipe and duct installers come first, their systems
get tested, and only then does insulation go on — and then the ceiling closes
over it, or the wall goes up in front of it.

This position has one consequence that shapes the whole job, so it is worth
stating plainly. **You are almost always working against a date that somebody
else set and that does not move.** The ceiling grid subcontractor is booked. If
the pipework ran late, the time comes out of your window, not theirs. Section
01.8 covers this from the new-build side; see
[01.8.01](../ch01/01.8.01-how-a-building-goes-up-and-where-insulation-falls-in-the-seq.md).

The other consequence is 隠ぺい — your work disappears. The standard
specification for mechanical services on government building work requires the
contractor to keep records and photographs of work that later inspection cannot
see, and specifically names work that becomes concealed as work that has to be
recorded. Once the ceiling is closed, the photograph is the only evidence that
the joint underneath was done properly. See
[01.9.03](../ch01/01.9.03-photographing-work-that-will-be-hidden.md).

## Worked example

The numbers below are an illustration, not a measured figure from any source.
The arithmetic is the point.

A floor of an office building. The programme says:

- Pipework and ductwork installation complete on the 12th
- Pressure testing on the 13th, 14th and 15th
- Insulation from the 16th to the 19th
- Ceiling grid starts on the 20th

That gives four days for insulation. Now the pipe crew hits a problem and
finishes on the 14th instead of the 12th. Testing moves to the 15th, 16th and
17th. The ceiling grid start on the 20th does not move, because the ceiling
subcontractor is on another job from the 21st.

Insulation now has two days for four days of work. The delay was two days, and
half of your window is gone.

Nobody will describe this to you as a problem caused by the pipe crew. It will
arrive as a request to get the floor done by the 19th. Understanding where the
two days went does not get them back, but it does tell you what to do: say early
and clearly that four days of work will not fit into two, so that the person who
can move the 20th finds out while there is still time to move it. Saying it on
the evening of the 19th helps nobody.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the programme as somebody else's business.** The dates decide what your week looks like. Ask what the ceiling-closing date is on the area you are working in; it is the single most useful number on a services job.
- **Assuming an instruction that reaches your crew has reached everyone.** It normally has not. The chain delivers information down one branch at a time, and a change that reached your supervisor at seven o'clock may not reach the crew on the floor above until the following morning.
- **Staying quiet about a date you already know you will miss.** A problem raised three days early is a scheduling matter. The same problem raised on the afternoon of the deadline is a failure. Nothing about the work changed in between.
- **Skipping 片付け because the work itself is finished.** On a shared site the condition you leave an area in is the most visible thing your company produces. It is also what stops the next trade damaging your work by shoving it aside.
- **Treating the daily patrol as an inspection to hide from.** It is a legal duty of the prime contractor, it happens on every site, and it happens to everybody. Answer the question you are asked.
- **Confusing the client's supervising officer with your supervisor.** On public work the 監督職員 checks the work against the specification. Instructions to you come through your own company, and an instruction that arrives from anywhere else is a thing to report rather than to act on.
- **Thinking of 段取り as wasted time.** On small-crew, multi-site work, getting the right material to the right floor is most of what makes a day productive. A crew that starts cutting at eight and runs out of banding at ten has not saved anything.

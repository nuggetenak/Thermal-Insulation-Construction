# Completion and handover

## What it is

[01.3.01](../ch01/01.3.01-mobilization.md) opened this section on the point
that a job starts on paper before a van ever moves — the 実施工程表, the
施工計画書, the 施工図 all agreed before a tool is picked up. Completion and
引渡し, delivery of the finished work, is the same idea run in reverse: the
job does not end because the last section of pipe is wrapped, it ends when a
specific inspection has confirmed that and the work has actually been
delivered to whoever it is owed to. [01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md)
already covers the client's own inspection of the completed works under
specification clause 1.6.1, and hands the delivery side — what happens once
that inspection has passed, and what a subcontractor is actually owed once it
has — to this item.

## Why it matters on the job

The physical work finishing is not the same event as the job being over, and
the gap between the two is exactly where a crew's own interests sit. Getting
paid, being released from a site, being free to plan the next job all turn on
delivery having actually happened, not on the wrap looking finished. A worker
who assumes the job ends when the last run is covered has no reason to notice
whether the notice, the inspection and the delivery that are supposed to
follow have actually happened — and those three steps are where a prime
contractor's obligations to this employer's company, not to the individual
worker, are fixed by law.

## In detail

### The duty that governs this, and who it binds

建設業法第24条の4 (検査及び引渡し) is the strongest and most directly
actionable material behind this item, and it binds the **元請負人** — not the
crew standing on site. That word needs care: 建設業法第2条第5項 defines the
元請負人 as the party that orders work **under a subcontract**, so it is
whoever engaged your company, at whatever tier. On this employer's jobs that
is usually the mechanical services contractor above them, not the main
contractor at the top of the site. Reading it as the latter would put these
duties on a company your employer has no contract with, which is the opposite
of what the article does. The benefit of it runs to
this employer's company as the subcontractor, the way [01.3.01](../ch01/01.3.01-mobilization.md)
described 建設業法第24条の2 as a duty a worker would never guess exists
because it is exercised by someone above them.

第1項 sets the clock: when the 元請負人 receives notice from a subcontractor
that its work is complete, it must complete the inspection confirming that
completion within twenty days of receiving the notice — and as soon as it
actually can within that period, not by waiting out the full twenty as a
matter of course. The clock starts at the notice, not at the work stopping.
A crew that finishes wrapping on a Tuesday and does not tell anyone until
Friday has moved when the twenty days begins, not shortened the wait — the
days lost between finishing and reporting are the crew's own, gone before
the statutory clock even starts running.

第2項 covers what happens once that inspection confirms the work is done:
**where the subcontractor asks**, the prime contractor must take delivery of
the work immediately. This is not automatic — it follows a request — but once
that request is made and the inspection has passed, immediate delivery is
what the article requires.

### The proviso that changes what "immediate" actually means

第2項 carries a ただし, a proviso, and it is easy to flatten into the wrong
shape. The immediate-delivery duty in 第2項 does **not** apply where the
subcontract itself carries a special term — a 特約 — fixing delivery for a
stated day, so long as that day falls on or before the twentieth day after
the completion time the subcontract sets.

That is not the same as saying delivery happens within twenty days as a
general rule. The general rule is immediate delivery on request, once
inspection has passed. What the proviso does is let the subcontract itself
displace that general rule, but only within a bounded window — a fixed
delivery date the contract sets in advance, capped at twenty days past the
subcontract's own completion date. A contract that names a delivery date
within that window controls; a contract that says nothing about it falls
back to immediate delivery once the subcontractor asks. Either way, the
governing document is the subcontract this employer's company has signed,
not something a worker on site can read off the physical state of the run.

### A second completion notice, further up the chain

There is a completion notice in the specification too, and it is easy to
mistake for the one above. [01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) covers
clause 1.6.1(1) and the inspection date that follows it. The point worth
carrying here is its precondition: that notice may be given only where
**both** everything the 監督職員 instructed has actually been finished **and**
all of the 工事関係図書 the design documents require are complete. Paperwork
is not something that trails behind finished physical work — it is a
condition of being allowed to say the work is finished at all.

**But it is a different notice, between a different pair of parties.** Clause
1.6.1 is the main contractor telling the client's supervising officer that
the works under the main contract are complete. 建設業法第24条の4 is your
company telling whoever engaged it that its own package is complete. Neither
gates the other, and in the ordinary run of a job the sequence goes the other
way: subcontract packages are finished, inspected and delivered up the chain
before the contractor at the top is in any position to tell the client the
works are done. Do not wait on one to start the other.

The subcontract is not a mystery document either. 建設業法第19条第1項 requires
a construction contract to be put in writing and to state, among other things,
the timing and method of the inspection confirming completion and the time of
引渡し. So the 特約 the proviso above turns on is something the contract is
required to record — there is a document to ask about rather than a custom to
guess at.

### What actually gets handed over

What is submitted at completion is, under specification clause 1.7.1, stated
per job — 特記による. There is no single fixed list that applies to every
contract, and where the job's own documents are silent on the point, the
default that clause 1.7.1 falls back to is 1.7.2 完成図 and 1.7.3
保全に関する資料. 完成図 are completion drawings showing the work as it was actually
built, which may differ from the 施工図 it started from if anything changed
along the way. 保全に関する資料 is the handover pack proper: building
operating instructions, equipment instructions, performance test records,
the relevant filings, and the report from the 総合試運転調整 —
[01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) owns that term — the overall
commissioning and adjustment run carried out once installed equipment is
complete.

What matters for a reader on this employer's jobs is that this is a
**default**, not a fixed requirement to expect on every job. Whatever a
given contract's own documents say about what gets submitted at completion
governs that job specifically, and a worker has no way to know that from the
physical state of the work.

### What the specification does not cover

Handover itself — the physical act of a subcontractor's work passing to the
prime contractor — is not something the standard specification for
mechanical services work on public building projects governs. Its Part 1
general common provisions mention 引渡し only inside a definition of partial
delivery and inside the clause on arisings; nothing in it sets out how a
subcontractor's handover to the prime contractor above it actually happens.
That is not a gap in this account — it reflects where the duty genuinely
sits. What governs delivery for a company in this employer's position is
建設業法第24条の4, described above, and whatever the subcontract between
this employer's company and the prime contractor actually says. Treating the
specification as though it also settled a subcontractor's handover would be
stating a rule wider than any clause in it actually gives.

### What the last day on site usually looks like

For a van-based crew on a two-month job, "handover" is very rarely a
ceremony with a signature at a table. It is more often the last working day
on a given run: a walk-round of the finished work, and photographs already
taken weeks earlier, while the work was still open, under the practice
[01.9.03](../ch01/01.9.03-photographing-work-that-will-be-hidden.md) covers.
By the time the physical wrap is finished, the record of what is underneath
it — the joint, the weld, the support — already exists or it does not; there
is no second chance to make it at handover. Any snagging found on that
walk-round, and what a worker's own part in fixing it is, is a different
question, covered by [01.9.01](../ch01/01.9.01-what-snagging-is-and-why-insulation-gets-snagged.md)
for what snagging is, and by [01.3.05](../ch01/01.3.05-correction.md) for
what happens once something is found wrong. What separates handover from an
ordinary day is not a visible event on the physical run at all — it is the
notice, the inspection under 建設業法第24条の4第1項, and the delivery under
第2項, all of which happen above a crew's own line of sight, between the
company's own supervisor or owner and the prime contractor.

### What actually clears the room

[01.2.05](../ch01/01.2.05-housekeeping-expectations.md) already covers the
duty behind 後片付け under specification clause 1.3.11 — clearing up and
cleaning the parts of the works concerned once the works are complete — and
that clause is not restated here. What belongs to this item is only that
後片付け and 引渡し are two different things happening close together at the
end of a job: clearing up is a physical duty on the work itself, while
delivery is the legal event that actually closes the job out between the
subcontractor and the prime contractor. A tidy site is not the same thing as
a delivered one.

### What this means for a first-year worker in practice

None of the duties in 建設業法第24条の4 are exercised by a worker directly —
the notice, the inspection and the request for delivery are all decisions
made by the company's own supervisor or owner, in the same way
[01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) found that the person consented to
under clause 1.5.3 is rarely a first-year helper. What a worker's own part
actually is, distilled from the material above, is narrow but concrete:

- Not treating the physical work looking finished as the job being over.
  What starts the twenty-day clock is the notice being given, and that is a
  decision made above a worker's own position.
- Understanding that a delay in reporting a run as finished is not free —
  it delays the notice, which delays everything that follows it.
- Knowing that the paperwork behind a job — the 工事関係図書 clause 1.6.1
  requires, whatever a given contract's 特記 asks for under clause 1.7.1 — is
  not something to leave until later. It is a condition of the job being
  allowed to end at all.

### What is not settled here

Two real questions are not answered by anything consulted for this item, and
both are worth putting to a supervisor directly rather than guessing at.

**Whether snagging on this employer's jobs arrives as a written list, a
walk-round with the mechanical contractor, or a phone call** is not
established by anything consulted here. [01.9.01](../ch01/01.9.01-what-snagging-is-and-why-insulation-gets-snagged.md)
raises the same open question from the snagging side. A supervisor or the
company owner is the right person to ask, since the answer likely depends on
which mechanical contractor and which site a given job runs through.

**What a crew's own part in a handover actually is on a package this size**
is also not settled. On a large contract, delivery under 建設業法第24条の4
might plausibly be a visible event a crew is present for; whether that is
also true on an insulation subcontract this small, or whether it is
something the company's own supervisor or owner handles entirely off site
after the crew has already moved on to the next job, is not addressed by
anything consulted here. That is a reasonable question to put to the
supervisor directly, not something this item can settle on its own.

One limit that applies throughout: the specification cited here governs public building work, and while private jobs usually follow it, usually is not always — the document your own job works to is the one that binds it. See [01.3.01](../ch01/01.3.01-mobilization.md).

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the physical work being finished as the job being over.** 建設業法第24条の4第1項's twenty-day clock starts at the notice that the work is complete, not at the moment the last section is wrapped. A crew that finishes and says nothing has not shortened anything — it has only delayed the point the clock starts.
- **Reading the 第2項 proviso as "delivery within twenty days" as a general rule.** The general rule is immediate delivery once the subcontractor asks and inspection has passed. A subcontract term can only displace that within a bounded window — a stated delivery date on or before twenty days after the subcontract's own completion time — and only where the subcontract actually says so.
- **Assuming the specification governs how a subcontractor's handover happens.** Its Part 1 general common provisions do not set out that process at all; what governs it is 建設業法第24条の4 and the subcontract itself.
- **Treating 1.7.2 and 1.7.3 as a fixed submission list on every job.** Specification clause 1.7.1 makes what is submitted at completion a matter stated per job, with those two only as the fallback where the job's own documents are silent.
- **Confusing a tidy site with a delivered one.** 後片付け under clause 1.3.11 is a physical duty on the work itself. Delivery under 建設業法第24条の4第2項 is the separate legal event that actually closes the job out between the subcontractor and the prime contractor.

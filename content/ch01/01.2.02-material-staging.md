# Material staging

## What it is

Material staging is everything that happens to a delivery between the truck
dropping it and someone picking it up to install it. That includes reporting
that it arrived, checking it is the right thing and not damaged, finding
somewhere for it to sit, and keeping it in usable condition while it waits.
None of that is automatic, and none of it is somebody else's job by default.

On a large new-build job, staging has a name and a place: a fenced 資材置場
inside the compound, with a gate and someone who signs deliveries in. On the
work this trade actually does — a corridor in a working hospital, a plant
room reached through a ward, a classroom during the holidays — there is
usually no such place. There is a spot someone agreed to, and the questions
that matter are less about the spot than about who agreed to it and for how
long.

## Why it matters on the job

Two failures show up over and over, and neither is about carelessness with
the material itself.

The first is material that sits unreported or uninspected until it is
already being cut. A short delivery, a box that got wet in transit, the
wrong bore of pipe section — any of these is a same-day problem if caught at
the door, and a lost afternoon if caught halfway through installation.

The second is a pile that turns into somebody else's hazard. A staging spot
that made sense for a two-day job becomes a stack of pipe sections against a
corridor wall on day three, still there when a porter comes through with a
bed. Nobody decided to create that hazard. It accumulated, because nobody
was assigned to decide when the spot had stopped being reasonable.

## In detail

### Report it, inspect it, and know what happens when it fails

On public building work the specification sets out a short chain for every
delivery. Clause 1.4.3 requires every delivery of plant and materials to be
reported to the 監督職員, the client's supervising officer, unless prior
agreement has already removed the need. Clause 1.4.4 then has that material
inspected by type — once a type has passed, later deliveries of the same
type can move to sampling rather than a full check — and states plainly that
**material which fails inspection is taken off site immediately**. Clause
1.4.6 covers what happens after it passes: it is stored so that it is not
damaged or degraded before use, and material later judged unfit through
damage or degradation is, again, taken off site immediately.

That chain assumes a supervising officer checking deliveries against a
schedule, the public-work picture. A six-person subcontractor is unlikely to
have anyone with that title standing at the door. What carries over
regardless is the shape of the duty: somebody on the crew has to actually
look at what arrived, compare it to what was ordered, and be willing to say a
damaged box is not going into the wall. Whose job that is on a given crew is
worth asking a supervisor directly rather than assuming it happens by default.

### 養生 — protecting what is already there

Clause 1.3.10 requires existing parts of the building and completed parts of
the works to be protected so they are not soiled or damaged. That duty is
called 養生 in Japanese practice, and it runs in both directions from a
staging pile: protecting the floor and the wall the material sits against
from the material, and protecting the material from a floor that is being
mopped, a corridor that carries foot traffic, or weather coming through an
open loading door. A stack of glass wool sections left against an unprotected
wall can mark or dent the finish underneath it as easily as a careless cut
can damage the material itself.

### Where a pile is allowed to sit

Reporting and protecting a delivery still leaves the physical question of
where it goes, and that question has a hard limit behind it. The
労働安全衛生規則, the regulations governing workplace safety generally, sets
requirements for an indoor passage at 第542条: its width has to match how
it is actually used, its surface has to be kept free of tripping, slipping
and punch-through hazards, and **nothing may be placed within 1.8 m above
the passage surface**.

Read that last one precisely, because it is easy to turn into the wrong rule.
It is headroom **over the passage** — something placed in the space people walk
through. It is not a height limit on a stack standing beside a corridor. A pile
against the wall is governed instead by the first requirement and by 第540条,
which requires a passage to be kept effective at all times: does it still have
a width matched to its use, and can people still use it? A 1.7 m stack that
squeezes a corridor to single file fails those and clears the 1.8 m; a 1.9 m
stack in an alcove out of the way is the other way round. Measuring the stack
is the wrong instinct — look at what is left of the passage.

Whether these clauses reach a hospital's own corridor that the job is merely
borrowing is worth being honest about. They are written about the passages an
employer provides in its own workplace, and no source consulted here settles
whether they bind a subcontractor in respect of a corridor it neither built nor
controls. Treating a borrowed corridor to the same standard is the safe
reading, and it is a reading rather than a settled rule.

Outside the building, 建設工事公衆災害防止対策要綱の建築工事等編, the outline for
preventing harm to third parties who have nothing to do with the job,
carries the same instinct. 第14 requires the work area and its surroundings
to be kept tidy at all times, specifically so that dust does not become a
nuisance to people nearby. 第22 requires the delivery route itself to be
surveyed in advance — whether a van can reach the loading door, or whether
material has to be hand-carried through an occupied lobby, is not something
to work out for the first time when the truck arrives.

### Flammable material needs its own store, not a corner

Adhesives, solvents and any gas cylinders on the job do not belong in the
same pile as everything else. 第19 of that same outline requires flammable
paints, oils and other flammable material, along with cylinders, to be kept
in a dedicated store: out of direct sunlight, ventilated, marked 危険物
(dangerous goods) and 火気厳禁 (no naked flames), with an appointed person
responsible for it. A tin of contact adhesive tucked into a corner of the
corridor staging spot, with nobody named as responsible for it, is the common
case on a small job rather than the exception, and closing that gap is a
question for the supervisor — where the adhesive and any cylinders are
supposed to sit, and whether that spot exists on this particular site.

### For a van-based crew, staging is usually two places, not one

None of the above assumes a laydown yard, and for a two-person crew doing a
day trip it should not. In practice there are two staging areas on a job
like this: the van, and one agreed spot inside the building, if the day's
work needs more material on hand than fits in a couple of trips from the
van. See [01.7.02](../ch01/01.7.02-loading-out-for-the-day-getting-materials-to-the-job.md)
for how material gets from the yard into the van in the first place.

The indoor spot is only as good as the agreement behind it. It needs to be
somewhere a named person — the site contact, a ward manager, whoever is
actually responsible for that part of the building — has said yes to,
because a spot nobody agreed to is exactly the pile that ends up against a
corridor wall with no protection under it and no plan for moving it.
Whether that agreement is expected to be written down anywhere, or is
normally just a verbal understanding from the morning meeting, is not
established by any source consulted here; see
[01.2.01](../ch01/01.2.01-restricted-areas.md) for the same uncertainty
about how a corridor closure gets recorded.

What goes back in the van at the end of the day is a separate decision from
what gets staged for tomorrow, and leaving material overnight inside
somebody else's building depends on whether anywhere lockable has been
offered, and whether the space is needed by another trade first thing the
next morning. Neither of those is settled by any source consulted here.

### What is not settled here

Whether a hot-work permit is issued before adhesive is used near an ignition
source, or before any cutting that could throw sparks, and what that permit
looks like on this employer's jobs, is not stated by any source consulted
here — ask the supervisor before assuming either way. Whether offcuts and
packaging staged during the day go back to the yard in the van or into the
prime contractor's own skip is governed by law rather than by convenience —
see [01.2.04](../ch01/01.2.04-tool-and-waste-zones.md), which sets out why the
yard is generally not one of the two options — but the actual practice at this
company is not established here. And whether another trade can
simply move a staged pile it needs the room for, or has to ask first, is not
answered by anything read for this item; if material goes missing or gets
damaged after being moved by someone else, that is a conversation for the
supervisor, not a guess to make on site.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating an unreported delivery as staged material rather than an open question.** Clause 1.4.3 makes reporting the first step, not an afterthought done once someone gets around to it. A pallet nobody told the site about is a pallet nobody has actually checked.
- **Installing from a pile that was never inspected.** Clause 1.4.4 exists because damaged or wrong material found after it is in the wall costs far more than the same material found at the door. If nobody on the crew is checking deliveries against the order, that is worth raising directly.
- **Letting a pile grow past the 1.8 m clearance without noticing.** 第542条 sets an exact limit above an indoor passage, and a stack that started well under it can pass it two deliveries later without anyone deciding it should.
- **Storing adhesive or solvent wherever there is space.** 第19 of the building volume requires a dedicated, ventilated store marked 危険物 and 火気厳禁 with a named person responsible, where dangerous goods are stored at the work area — and it operates through fire law, which regulates by quantity. Whether a day's adhesive carried in and out on the van ever reaches that point is not something this item settles; cylinders are the clear case. Ask where they are meant to live rather than assuming any corner will do.
- **Assuming the agreed staging spot is still agreed to on day four.** A spot a ward manager approved for a two-day job is not automatically still available on day four, and another trade needing that room does not always announce it in advance. Check rather than assume the pile can stay where it is.
- **Confusing wet-damaged material with what a laboratory humidity test measured.** The moisture figures sometimes quoted for glass wool describe several hours in a sealed high-humidity chamber, not a bag left in the rain — see [02.3.01](../ch02/02.3.01-why-insulation-reduces-heat-flow.md) rather than assuming rain-soaked material is somewhere near that number.

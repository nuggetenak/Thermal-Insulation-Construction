# Preparation

## What it is

Preparation is the gap between arriving at a task and actually cutting or
wrapping insulation on it. Mobilization, covered in
[01.3.01](../ch01/01.3.01-mobilization.md), gets a crew and its material onto a
site. Preparation is narrower and happens over and over through a working
day: before a specific run of pipe or a specific duct section is insulated,
someone has to confirm what it actually is, what drawing governs it, and
whether it is genuinely ready — pressure-tested, fixed in its final position,
free of anything that still needs to happen to it first.

Little of this rests on a clause with a figure in it. Most of it is how the
work is actually carried out, learned by watching a supervisor do it before
being trusted to do it alone. This item says plainly which pieces are backed
by a document and which are trade practice, rather than dressing practice up
to sound like law.

## Why it matters on the job

The trade covers its own evidence. Once a pipe or duct is wrapped, the weld,
the joint, the support and the surface underneath it are gone from view for
years. Insulating something that was not actually ready — a joint not yet
pressure-tested, a run built to a drawing that was later changed — does not
produce a visible mistake the way a crooked shelf does. It produces a defect
sealed inside a finished wall of insulation, found only when something leaks
or when someone finally has to cut it open.

That is also why the crew, and not only the supervisor, needs to know before
starting whether a piece of work falls into a category someone else is
supposed to see before it disappears. A worker who understands that some work
gets an inspection specifically because it is about to become impossible to
inspect will ask, rather than assume silence means proceed.

## In detail

### Working from the drawing that is actually current

The specification for mechanical services work on public building projects
— cited here from its Part 1 general common provisions, since the document
reuses clause numbers across its later parts — sets out at clause 1.5.1 that
the work is carried out on the basis of the design documents, the
実施工程表, the 施工計画書 and the 施工図. That clause is the spine
[01.3.01](../ch01/01.3.01-mobilization.md) already covers. What belongs here
is the consequence at the point a tool is about to be picked up: the drawing
governing a run is a controlled document, produced before the work it covers
and needing the 監督職員's consent under clause 1.2.3, and a drawing that has
since been superseded by a change is worse than having no drawing at all,
because it reads as authoritative while being wrong.

Reading the run before cutting anything means finding the current version of
the drawing for that specific piece of work, not the version remembered from
a briefing the day before or carried on a phone from an earlier visit. What a
superseded drawing actually looks like on this trade's jobs — whether a
revision is called out by date, by a revision letter, or some other way — is
not addressed by anything cited here; confirming which drawing is current for
a given task is a question for the supervisor, and
[04.5.04](../ch04/04.5.04-never-guessing-from-outdated-drawings.md) covers
the failure mode of working from an out-of-date sheet directly.

### Confirming what has actually happened to the pipe before it is covered

Insulating a joint that has not been pressure-tested, or a system that has
not passed its leak testing, buries the one chance anyone had to catch a
fault before it was hidden. The clauses governing 水圧試験 and 気密試験, and
what has to happen before a run is covered, belong to
[01.1.03](../ch01/01.1.03-relationship-between-piping-hvac-electrical-fire-protection-.md)
and are not restated here. What belongs to preparation is the discipline of
checking, before starting, that the specific run in front of the crew has
actually cleared that stage — not assuming it has because the crew was told
the system as a whole was tested, and not assuming a green tag or a chalk
mark seen on a similar run nearby applies to this one.

### Knowing in advance whether this work is watched

Clause 1.5.8 of the specification requires the 監督職員 to attend the work
in several listed cases: where the design documents call for it, where main
equipment is being installed, where the overall commissioning and adjustment
run is being carried out, where the 監督職員
specifically instructs, and — the case that matters most for this trade —
**where work is being done that is difficult to inspect once it is
finished.** Attendance has to be requested in good time, and it is the
contractor's job to provide what that attendance needs. What that attendance
actually looks like on the ground, and what a crew does if it starts work
without knowing whether this run falls into that category, belongs to
[01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) rather than to preparation. The
point that belongs here is narrower: because insulation work covers its own
evidence almost by definition, a crew that has not asked whether a given run
needs to be seen before it is wrapped is gambling with a clause that exists
specifically to stop that gamble.

### The guidance a crew is expected to follow

建設業法第26条の4第2項 places a duty directly on the people doing the work:
they must follow the guidance the 主任技術者 gives as part of that role —
drawing up the plan for carrying out the work, controlling the programme,
controlling quality, and giving technical direction and supervision on site.
That duty is stated at the width the article gives it. It is about following
the 主任技術者's guidance in that specific role, not a general rule that
anyone more senior on a site must be obeyed regardless of what they are
directing. On a six-person company the 主任技術者 may be the person standing
next to the crew that morning, which is what makes the duty concrete rather
than a distant legal abstraction — the person giving the instruction to
re-check a joint before wrapping it is very often exercising exactly this
role, not merely being cautious.

### The parts of preparation that are practice, not law

Before any of the above happens, the crew still needs the material for that
specific task on hand and a way to actually reach the work — staged where it
will not block a corridor someone else needs, and accessed safely, both
covered in [01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md) and
[01.2.03](../ch01/01.2.03-access-routes.md) rather than here. And before any
cutting starts, a KY discussion — 危険予知, spotting the hazards specific to
this task — and the day's 段取り, the setting-up that makes the work
possible, both belong to
[01.1.01](../ch01/01.1.01-general-construction-workflow.md). None of that is
a specification clause. It is how the work is actually organized on a small
crew, and it happens before every task, not only at the start of a job.

What has not been established anywhere consulted here is how formally any of
this gets confirmed on a job this size — whether a supervisor walks the run
with the crew before work starts, whether confirming the drawing is current
is a spoken check or something written down, or whether it is left to the
worker doing the task to raise a concern if something looks wrong. That is a
reasonable thing to ask a supervisor directly rather than to assume.

### What is not settled here

Who signs off that a stage of work is genuinely ready to proceed — whether
that is the crew's own supervisor, the mechanical contractor above them, or
someone confirming against the 監督職員's expectations — is not established
by anything consulted here on a package this small. Neither is whether this
company's crews ever produce their own per-trade plan for a piece of work, or
whether they work entirely to a plan the mechanical contractor above them has
already written; on a job this size the second looks more plausible, but
nothing consulted here confirms it either way. And what a first hour on an
ordinary working day actually looks like for this employer — what gets
checked before anyone picks up a tool, and in what order — is not written
down anywhere consulted here. All three are questions for the crew's own
supervisor or the company owner.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a drawing carried from memory or from an earlier visit.** A 施工図 that has since been superseded is worse than having no drawing, because it looks authoritative while being wrong. Confirm the current version before cutting or wrapping anything.
- **Assuming a run has been pressure-tested because the system as a whole was.** A tag or chalk mark seen on a nearby run does not confirm the specific joint in front of the crew. Check the run being worked on, not the neighbourhood.
- **Starting a run that is difficult to inspect afterward without asking whether it needs to be seen first.** Clause 1.5.8 exists precisely because this trade's finished work hides the evidence underneath it. Not knowing the answer is a reason to ask, not a reason to proceed.
- **Treating 建設業法第26条の4第2項 as a general rule that anyone senior must be obeyed.** It binds the crew to the 主任技術者's guidance in that specific role — drawing up the plan, controlling quality and programme, giving technical direction — not to any instruction from anyone with more experience.
- **Reading preparation as a single event at the start of a job rather than something repeated before every task.** Confirming the drawing, the test status and the day's 段取り happens before each run, not once at mobilization and then never again.

# Inspection

## What it is

The word "inspection" gets used loosely on a job, and on this trade it covers
at least four different things that happen at different times, are carried
out by different people, and follow from different clauses of the standard
specification for mechanical services work on public building projects,
cited here from its Part 1 general common provisions since the document
reuses clause numbers across its later parts. [01.3.03](../ch01/01.3.03-installation.md)
introduced the 一工程 — a defined stage of the work, not the whole job — and
left the mechanics of what gets confirmed at the end of one to here. This
item lays the four apart, because collapsing them into a single idea of
"getting checked" is exactly what leads a worker to miss the one that matters
most for this trade.

## Why it matters on the job

Three of the four things this item covers happen while work is still going
and can still be corrected cheaply. The fourth happens only after everything
else is already finished. Confusing which is which has a real cost: a worker
who thinks a joint has been seen by someone because a supervisor walked past
it may be wrong about who actually confirmed it, and a worker who does not
know that a run falls into the category of work that has to be watched while
it happens has no way to ask for that attendance before it is too late to
give.

[01.3.02](../ch01/01.3.02-preparation.md) already makes the point that this
trade's finished work covers its own evidence — a wrapped pipe hides the
weld, the joint and the support underneath it for years. That single fact is
the reason the specification treats attendance during the work, not only
inspection of the finished result, as something a worker needs to think about
before starting a run rather than after finishing it.

## In detail

### 1. The crew's own confirmation, reported upward — clause 1.5.3

On completing a 一工程, or when the 監督職員 — the client's supervising
officer on public work — gives an instruction during it, specification
clause 1.5.3 requires the work to be confirmed against the design documents
and reported to the 監督職員 in good time. This is the first and most
frequent of the four, because it happens at the end of every stage the job
is divided into, not once at the end of the project.

The clause names who does this precisely: **the confirmation and the report
are made by a person the 監督職員 has given consent to in advance**, not by
whoever on the crew happens to be free at the moment a stage finishes. A
first-year helper wrapping a run is very rarely that person. What follows
from that is not that the confirmation is someone else's problem to ignore —
it is that a helper's actual contribution to clause 1.5.3 is making sure the
person who is consented to actually gets to see the work before it is
covered, which circles back to knowing, before starting, whether a given run
needs to be watched while it happens rather than checked after.

### 2. The 監督職員's own inspection of the work — clause 1.5.4

Separately from the crew's own confirmation, clause 1.5.4 gives the 監督職員
an inspection of the work itself, carried out where the design documents
require it or where a report was made under clause 1.5.3. Once a piece of
work has passed that inspection, the clause allows later parts built with
the same materials and the same method to move to sampling inspection rather
than being inspected individually every time — but the clause carries its own
limit on that shortcut: not where the 監督職員 instructs otherwise.

That proviso matters more than it looks. A crew that reads "one pass and the
rest gets sampled" without the limit has learned a rule the clause does not
actually give — the 監督職員 can require full inspection to continue on any
later run, for any reason of its own, regardless of how the first run went.
Nothing about a pass on the first section of a run guarantees the same
treatment for the tenth.

### 3. Attendance while the work is being done — clause 1.5.8

This is the one that matters most for this trade, and it is a genuinely
different thing from the two above: it is not an inspection of finished
work at all, but the 監督職員 being physically present **while the work is
still happening**. Clause 1.5.8 requires that attendance in five listed
cases: where the design documents call for it; where main equipment is being
installed; where the 総合試運転調整 — the overall commissioning and
adjustment run — is being carried out; where the 監督職員 specifically
instructs; and, the case that reaches this trade directly, **where work is
being done that is difficult to inspect once it is finished.**

[01.3.02](../ch01/01.3.02-preparation.md) already makes the point that this
trade's output is exactly that kind of work — once insulation is wrapped,
what is underneath it is gone from view. What belongs here is what that
means in practice for a worker on the day. Attendance under this clause has
to be requested in good time, and it is the contractor's job to provide
whatever that attendance actually needs — access, timing, whatever the
監督職員 asks for to be able to see the joint or the support before it
disappears. A helper cannot request that attendance themselves; that is a
decision made above them, through the supervisor. What a helper can and
should do is know, before starting to wrap a given run, whether it is the
kind of run this clause is written for, and say so if nobody seems to have
asked. A run started and finished without that question being raised cannot
be un-covered afterward — the chance is genuinely gone, not merely
inconvenient to recover.

This is not the same event as clause 1.5.3's confirmation. Clause 1.5.3 is
the crew reporting that a stage is done; clause 1.5.8 is the 監督職員
standing there while the work happens, because waiting until it is done
would be too late to see it properly. A job can involve both on the same
run, and neither substitutes for the other.

### 4. The client's inspection at the end of the works — clause 1.6.1

The fourth is different in kind from the first three: it happens once,
after everything else is finished, and it belongs to the client rather than
to the day-to-day running of the job. Clause 1.6.1 sets a precondition on
even reaching that point. The notice that the works are complete may be
given to the 監督職員 **only where both** of two things hold at once:
everything the 監督職員 instructed has actually been finished, **and** all
of the 工事関係図書 — the construction-related documents the design
documents require — are complete. Inspection then happens on the date the
client notifies.

The second half of that condition is easy to underrate from the position of
someone doing the physical work. It is tempting to think of the paperwork
as something that gets tidied up after the real work is done. Clause 1.6.1
does not allow that order: the documents are not an afterthought trailing
the finished insulation, they are a condition of being allowed to say the
job is finished at all. A crew that has done flawless physical work but left
the paper trail incomplete has not reached the point where completion can be
declared. What actually happens at handover, and what those documents
consist of on a job of this size, belongs to
[01.3.06](../ch01/01.3.06-completion-and-handover.md) rather than here; the
point that belongs to inspection is narrower — this fourth kind is gated on
paperwork in a way none of the first three are.

### What a first-year helper actually does with all four

None of the four categories above is a decision a helper makes alone. The
person consented to under clause 1.5.3, the 監督職員 conducting a 1.5.4
inspection or a 1.5.8 attendance, and whoever assembles the 工事関係図書 for
clause 1.6.1 are all people above a helper in the chain. What a helper's own
job actually is, distilled from the four clauses above, is narrower:

- Not covering a run that has not been released to be covered — waiting for
  the confirmation clause 1.5.3 describes rather than assuming a stage is
  finished because the physical work looks done.
- Knowing, before starting to wrap a specific run, whether it is the kind of
  work clause 1.5.8 is written for — difficult to inspect once it is
  finished — and raising the question through the supervisor if nobody
  seems to have asked yet.
- Saying so when something looks wrong rather than wrapping it and hoping it
  gets caught later. Nothing in the specification consulted here gives a
  first-year helper the authority to stop a job on their own judgment, but
  the specification's whole structure — confirmation at every stage,
  attendance while work that will be hidden is done, a paperwork gate before
  completion can even be declared — exists because work like this is not
  safely left to be caught after the fact.

What has already been tested and is not restated here belongs to
[01.1.03](../ch01/01.1.03-relationship-between-piping-hvac-electrical-fire-protection-.md).
What actually gets photographed while a run is still open, and how, belongs
to [01.9.03](../ch01/01.9.03-photographing-work-that-will-be-hidden.md).
Inspection specifically before a ceiling closes over finished work belongs
to [01.9.02](../ch01/01.9.02-inspection-before-a-ceiling-is-closed.md). What
happens when an inspection under any of the four categories above finds
something wrong is a separate question, covered in
[01.3.05](../ch01/01.3.05-correction.md) rather than here.

### What is not settled here

Who actually confirms a completed 一工程 on an insulation package this
small — whether the crew's own supervisor, the mechanical contractor above
them, or someone reporting directly to the 監督職員's satisfaction — is not
established by anything consulted here, and [01.3.03](../ch01/01.3.03-installation.md)
raises the same open question. Neither is whether the 監督職員's consent to
that person under clause 1.5.3 is a visible, formal event on a job of this
scale, or something absorbed quietly into how the mechanical contractor
above this employer already runs its own process. Both are reasonable
questions to put to a supervisor directly, not something a specification
clause settles on its own for a crew this size.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating all four kinds of inspection as one thing.** A worker who thinks "getting checked" is a single event misses that clause 1.5.8 attendance happens while the work is still under way and cannot be recovered afterward, while clause 1.5.4 inspection happens once the work is already there to be looked at.
- **Reading the clause 1.5.4 sampling shortcut without its proviso.** A pass on the first section does not guarantee sampling treatment for every later one — the 監督職員 can require full inspection on any later run regardless of how earlier ones went.
- **Wrapping a run without asking whether clause 1.5.8 applies to it.** Once insulation is on, the chance to have it watched while it was being installed is gone permanently, not merely delayed.
- **Assuming the physical work being finished means the job is finished.** Clause 1.6.1 makes the notice of completion conditional on the 工事関係図書 being complete as well as the instructed work — the paperwork is a gate, not a formality trailing behind the real work.
- **Assuming a stage has been confirmed because a supervisor was seen nearby.** Clause 1.5.3's confirmation and report are made by a specific person the 監督職員 has consented to in advance, not by anyone present at the time.

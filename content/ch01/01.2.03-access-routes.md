# Access routes

## What it is

An access route is the path a worker and their material actually take to get
from where the van is parked to where the work is, and back again. On a
fenced new-build site that path might run entirely inside a boundary nobody
else uses. On this trade's work it usually does not. The route from the van
to a hospital plant room, a school boiler house or an office ceiling void
crosses a lobby, a stairwell, a ward corridor or a loading dock that belongs
to the building and its own occupants first, and to the job second. The
Japanese term for a route in general is 通路; where a purpose-built temporary
structure is provided to get people over or around an obstacle, it is a
架設通路. The specific line a trolley or a hand-carried bundle of pipe
sections actually follows on a given day is its 動線, and it is worth
thinking about deliberately rather than discovering by trial.

## Why it matters on the job

A blocked or unsafe passage is not a minor inconvenience on this trade's
sites — it is the one hazard nearly every job shares, because nearly every
job involves carrying material and tools through somebody else's building.
Three separate things can go wrong on the same fifty metres of corridor: the
route itself can be unsafe to use (poor lighting, a trip hazard, an
overhead obstruction), it can be blocked by the job's own material or
equipment, or it can be a route the building's own occupants need at a
moment nobody on the crew controls — a fire escape, a route to a ward bay, a
route a porter needs for a bed. Getting access routes wrong does not usually
look dramatic. It looks like a trolley left across a doorway for two
minutes, and it is exactly that kind of small, ordinary lapse that this item
exists to head off.

## In detail

### The passage rules that apply everywhere

The 労働安全衛生規則, the regulations governing workplace safety generally,
set out passage rules that are not written for construction sites
specifically — they apply to any workplace, which is why they reach a
corridor inside an occupied building as much as a new-build site. 第540条
requires safe passages to and within the workplace, kept effective **at all
times**, with the main ones marked so people can see they are passages.
第541条 requires enough daylight or lighting that normal passage is not
hindered. 第542条 sets the indoor passage rules directly relevant to this
trade: width matched to how the passage is actually used, a surface kept
free of tripping, slipping and punch-through hazards, and nothing placed
within 1.8 m above it. What that clause means for a material pile against a
corridor wall is covered in
[01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md) rather than repeated here —
what matters for access is that the same clause governs whether the passage
itself stays walkable while material is moving through it, not only while it
sits still.

第543条 governs a passage provided between machines, or between a machine and
other equipment: where a gap is there to be walked through, it has to be at
least 80 cm wide. Read it carefully — it sets the width of a passage, not a
spacing rule for every pair of machines, so a narrow gap nobody routes people
through is not in breach by itself. Where the gap is the way through, it is
the clause that bites in a crowded plant room: a boiler, a pump skid and a
length of scaffold left leaning against the wall can eat that width fast, and
a worker carrying a pipe section through it is the one who finds out. 第544条 requires the floor of a
work area itself to be kept free of tripping and slipping hazards, which
covers the plant room floor once a worker is standing in it, distinct from
the passage that got them there.

### Temporary walkways, and whose responsibility they are

Where the route to the work is not an ordinary corridor but a purpose-built
temporary structure — a ramp over an obstruction, a walkway bridging a
trench, boards laid across an unfinished floor — that structure is a
架設通路, and 第552条 sets what it has to be: sound construction; a slope of
30 degrees or less, unless it is a stairway or is under 2 m in rise with a
firm handhold; anti-slip treads where the slope exceeds 15 degrees; and,
wherever there is a risk of falling, guardrails at 85 cm or higher with an
intermediate rail set between 35 cm and 50 cm.

第654条 matters specifically because this trade is nearly always a
subcontractor working inside somebody else's building. Where the party that
ordered the work lets a contractor's workers use a temporary walkway, that
party is the one legally required to make it conform to 第552条. In practice
that means a walkway a crew is simply handed — boards over an opening, a
ramp put in by another trade, a temporary bridge across scaffolding — is
somebody else's legal responsibility to have built correctly, not a
structure to inspect and repair on the spot. A walkway that looks
undersized, unrailed where there is a drop, or built without anti-slip
treads on a steep run is worth reporting to the supervisor rather than using
carefully. Using it carefully does not fix a walkway that fails 第552条; it
only delays finding out that it does.

### Outside the building, where the public is

建設工事公衆災害防止対策要綱, the outline for preventing harm to third
parties who have nothing to do with the job, sets figures for a pedestrian
route at 第33: at least 0.90 m wide, or 0.75 m where elderly people and
wheelchair users are not expected to use it, or 1.5 m where there are many
pedestrians; an effective height of at least 2.1 m; separated from the work
by fencing or panels; signed; lit at night; free of steps and unevenness;
kept slip-resistant; with ramps, handrails and tactile paving added where
needed.

第33 sits in the outline's traffic chapter, and it is written about
restricting a road or a public footway — the pavement outside the hospital
where the van is parked and unloaded, not the corridor inside the building.
It is the figure Japanese practice reaches for when a job narrows a route
the public actually walks along, on the street. Treating it as a rule for
an internal hospital corridor would be a mistake: nothing in the outline
extends those figures indoors, and citing 第33 for an indoor passage
misstates what the clause governs.

第17 of the same outline is directly useful indoors as well as out: where
existing lighting is temporarily removed or moved for the work, appropriate
lighting must be provided so that people passing are not impeded. Taking
down a light fitting to run insulation past it, or working in a corridor
with the lights switched off for the job, falls under this clause even
though the corridor is not a public road.

第22 requires the material delivery route to be surveyed in advance —
whether a van can reach the loading door directly, or material has to be
hand-carried through an occupied lobby, is something to establish before the
truck arrives, and [01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md) covers
that surveying duty for the delivery itself. The routing angle that belongs
here is what happens after the delivery is on site: which corridors, lifts
or stairwells the material then travels through to reach the work, and
whether that route needs its own agreement with the building separate from
the delivery point. Clause 1.3.6 of the specification puts the transport
plan for materials, the choice of route and the other vehicle-movement
questions on the contractor, to be worked out in coordination with the
relevant authorities with traffic safety as the object — a planning duty
that sits with whoever is running the job, not something a crew works out
by trial on the day.

### Crossing a building that is still in use

The corridor a crew crosses to reach a plant room is a fire escape route for
people who are not on the job, and it stays one all day, every day the work
runs — not only during a fire. A trolley of pipe sections parked while a
worker fetches the next load, a ladder laid flat across a doorway for two
minutes, a length of hose run across a threshold: each of these is a route
the building's own occupants may need with no notice at all, and none of
those situations looks like an emergency until someone actually needs the
route and finds it blocked.

Whether a crew may use a building's passenger lift, use a goods lift, or has
to carry material up the stairs, and how a lift car gets protected if it is
used, is covered by [01.4.05](../ch01/01.4.05-access-routes-lifts-and-protecting-finished-surfaces.md)
rather than settled here.

Illuminance deserves care rather than a flat rule borrowed from elsewhere.
第604条 sets minimum illuminance at the working surface where people are
regularly employed — 300 lux for precise work, 150 lux for ordinary work,
70 lux for rough work. That clause describes a working surface used for
regular work, and it is not a rule about how much light is enough to move
along a route with a head torch in a ceiling void or a plant room being
worked in only occasionally. Using it as though it answered that question
would overstate what the clause covers; 第541条's passage-lighting
requirement above is the one that actually governs whether a route can be
walked safely.

### What is not settled here

Whether a passenger lift, a goods lift, or neither may be used by this trade
on a given site, and how a lift car is protected if it is used, is covered
by [01.4.05](../ch01/01.4.05-access-routes-lifts-and-protecting-finished-surfaces.md)
and is not settled here. Where the van may park and what the loading window
is at a given hospital — whether there is a set delivery bay, a time
restriction tied to ambulance access, or neither — is not established by
any source consulted here; it is a question for the site contact before the
first day of work. Which parts of a school are out of bounds during term
time, distinct from the holiday access this trade more often works, is
likewise not settled here and depends on the school's own arrangement with
the job.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the pedestrian-route figures of 第33 as a rule for an internal corridor.** That clause is written about restricting a public road or footway. Applying its width and separation figures to a hospital corridor overstates what it governs; the passage rules at 第540条 through 第542条 are the ones that actually apply indoors.
- **Treating a handed-over walkway as safe because it is already in place.** 第654条 puts the duty to make a temporary walkway conform to 第552条 on the party that ordered the work, not on the crew using it. A walkway that looks undersized or unrailed is worth reporting, not picking a careful path along.
- **Leaving a trolley or a length of ladder across a route "for a minute".** The corridor a crew crosses stays a fire escape route for the building's occupants all day, not only when nothing else is happening. A short block still counts as a block to whoever needs the route while it is there.
- **Assuming a working corridor has enough light because the plant room does.** 第541条 governs the passage itself; a well-lit plant room at the end of a dim corridor does not satisfy it, and where existing lighting has been taken down for the work, 第17 of the public-hazard outline requires it to be replaced with something adequate.
- **Quoting 第604条's illuminance figures for a route rather than a working surface.** That clause is about the surface where regular work happens, not about how much light is needed to move safely along a corridor or through a ceiling void.

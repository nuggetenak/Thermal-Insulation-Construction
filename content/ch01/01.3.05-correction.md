# Correction

## What it is

Correction is what happens after someone decides that a piece of installed
insulation is wrong — the wrong material, the wrong thickness, a joint that
was never finished, a run wrapped over a defect it should have caught first.
[01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) covers who checks the work and when.
This item covers the narrower question of what follows when the answer to one
of those checks is no.

It is worth saying plainly at the start that this is the thinnest item in
this section for citable rules. The standard specification for mechanical
services work on public building projects has plenty to say about inspecting
work and almost nothing to say about fixing it once it is found wrong.
Searching its Part 1 general common provisions for a clause on correcting
defective installed work turns up nothing. What exists instead is a single
provision about **materials** that fail inspection, a duty on larger
contractors to correct legal violations in a subcontractor's conduct — which
is not the same thing as fixing bad work — and, beyond that, whatever the
reader's own contract says. This item describes how the situation is
actually handled in practice, and says clearly where that description is
not backed by a source.

## Why it matters on the job

Insulation covers its own evidence. A joint wrapped over a problem does not
look wrong from the outside — it looks finished. That is exactly why the
single most valuable habit for a new worker is reporting a mistake early,
before it is wrapped and before the crew has left the building. A defect
caught by the worker who made it, on the same day, is a five-minute fix. The
same defect found by someone else weeks later, after the jacketing is on and
the crew is back home, is a return trip, an argument about whose fault it
was, and a mark against the crew's name with the prime contractor — a trust
problem layered on top of the work problem. Nothing about hiding a mistake
makes it smaller. It only moves the discovery to a worse time.

## In detail

### Who finds it

A defect in installed insulation can surface in more than one way, and each
carries a different weight. The 監督職員's own inspection of the work under
specification clause 1.5.4 — covered in
[01.3.04](../ch01/01.3.04-inspection.md) and not restated here — is the
formal route on a public job, and it can happen either during the work or
afterward. A following trade can also find it: an electrician or a plumber
working the same space notices insulation missing over a joint they need
access to. A supervisor walking a run can spot it on sight. And the worker
who installed it can notice something is wrong before anyone else does —
a section that does not sit right, a joint rushed at the end of a shift, a
material pulled from the wrong pile.

Of those four, the last is the one worth building a habit around. The other
three depend on someone else being in the right place at the right time,
which on a small crew working a room alone is not guaranteed for weeks. The
worker who did the work is the one person certain to have seen it.

### Why saying so immediately is worth more than staying quiet

Reporting a mistake is a communication task as much as a work task, and how
to actually say it — describing what happened, in Japanese, to a supervisor
who did not see it — belongs to
[24.4.01](../ch24/24.4.01-what-happened.md) through
[24.4.05](../ch24/24.4.05-proposed-next-action.md) rather than here. What
belongs to correction is the reason it is worth doing at all: a supervisor
who hears about a problem the same day it happened can usually still send
someone back cheaply, or fold the fix into work already planned for that
building. A supervisor who finds out weeks later, from someone else, is
choosing between an expensive unplanned trip and leaving a known defect in
place — and is finding out, at the same time, that the crew that made the
mistake did not say anything.

### What correction actually costs on this kind of job

This employer's crews travel by van on day trips, sometimes across more than
one prefecture, to reach a site and return the same day —
[01.7.03](../ch01/01.7.03-day-trips-across-a-region-and-what-that-means-for-planning.md)
covers how that shape of work gets planned. A defect found after the crew has
already left is not a five-minute fix from where the supervisor is standing —
it is a full day lost to driving there and back for a job that might take an
hour once the crew arrives. Returning to a building that has already been
handed over as finished, rather than one still mid-job, carries its own
different considerations, covered in
[01.9.05](../ch01/01.9.05-returning-to-a-finished-building-to-fix-something.md).
Nothing consulted here states whether this employer treats a same-day defect
differently from one that surfaces after the crew has driven home, but the
travel pattern alone makes clear why the timing of a report changes what
fixing it actually costs.

### The one thing that is genuinely settled

Specification clause 1.4.4(3), in Part 1 of the standard specification —
cited from there because the document reuses clause numbers across its later
parts — is explicit about one case: **material** that fails inspection is
removed from the site immediately. That is a real rule with a real
consequence, and it is worth knowing. It is also narrow. It governs
material before it goes into a run, not work already installed. Nothing in
clause 1.4.4 or elsewhere in Part 1 extends that same immediate-removal
language to a joint or a section of pipe that was already wrapped and is
later found to be wrong. Treating 1.4.4(3) as though it also settled how
finished workmanship gets corrected would be stating a rule wider than the
clause actually gives it.

### A document that looks relevant and is not

建設業法第24条の7 is worth naming because a reader might reasonably expect
it to cover this ground, and it does not. The article places a duty on a
larger class of prime contractor to guide its subcontractors so they do not
breach the Construction Business Act or the employment laws listed alongside
it, to seek correction of a breach it finds, and — the one flat obligation
in the article — to report to the licensing authority where that correction
does not happen. That is correction of a **legal violation** in how a
subcontractor is being run, not correction of insulation that was installed
wrong. Reading it as a rework clause would be exactly the kind of inference
stated in a clause's own voice that this reference tries hard to avoid.

The rules that actually govern putting bad work right — 契約不適合責任 under
the Civil Code, and whatever the subcontract between this employer and the
prime contractor says about defects — are contract law, not something
carried by the specification or the Construction Business Act. Neither the
Civil Code nor this employer's subcontract is a document this reference has
read, so neither is described here beyond naming where the real answer
lives.

### Records, when a correction touches something already documented

Where the work being corrected is something that was already photographed or
recorded as concealed work under specification clause 1.2.4(4) —
[01.3.03](../ch01/01.3.03-installation.md) covers that clause, and
[01.9.03](../ch01/01.9.03-photographing-work-that-will-be-hidden.md) covers
how the photographing itself is actually done — a correction to it is worth
raising with whoever keeps those records, not just fixed and left. A record
that shows a joint as finished on a date before it was actually corrected is
a record that no longer matches what was built, and nothing consulted here
says how that gets reconciled on a job this size. That is a question for the
supervisor, not something to guess at.

### What is not settled here

Several real questions have no answer in anything consulted for this item,
and a reader should ask a supervisor rather than assume any of them:

- **How a correction actually gets recorded on this employer's jobs** — a
  written note, a verbal report that goes no further, or something else — is
  not established by anything consulted here.
- **Whether the crew that made a defect is the crew sent back to fix it**, or
  whether that decision depends on who is available and where the next job
  is, is not addressed by any source consulted.
- **Who bears the cost of a return trip** — the crew's own employer, folded
  into the original price, or something the prime contractor absorbs — is a
  commercial question this reference has no basis to answer.
- **Whether a defect found after handover is treated differently from one
  found mid-job** is a real distinction —
  [01.9.04](../ch01/01.9.04-defects-found-after-handover-and-who-pays.md)
  takes up who pays once the works have already been handed over — but
  nothing here settles how the two cases diverge in practice.

All four are questions for the crew's own supervisor or the company owner,
not something a specification clause or this reference can settle on its
own.

## Common mistakes

- **Wrapping over a known problem rather than reporting it.** A defect
  sealed inside finished insulation is not smaller than one reported on the
  spot — it is the same defect, discovered later, at a worse time, after the
  crew that could have fixed it cheaply has already left the building.
- **Treating clause 1.4.4(3) as though it covered installed work.** It
  requires material that fails inspection to leave the site immediately.
  Nothing in Part 1 of the specification extends that same rule to
  workmanship already installed and later found wrong.
- **Citing 建設業法第24条の7 as the rule that requires rework.** It is a duty
  about correcting legal violations in how a subcontractor operates, not a
  clause about fixing bad insulation. The real ground for demanding rework is
  the contract and 契約不適合責任 under the Civil Code, neither of which is
  described here beyond naming it.
- **Assuming silence from a supervisor means the fix can wait.** A supervisor
  who does not know about a problem cannot plan around it. Saying nothing is
  not the same as being told to leave it.
- **Fixing a correction and never mentioning it to whoever keeps the site
  records.** Where the original work was photographed or logged as concealed
  work, a later correction that nobody records leaves that log describing
  something that no longer matches what was actually built.

# Subcontractor and site organization basics

## What it is

On a construction site there are two separate structures running at the same
time, and confusing them is the commonest organisational mistake a new worker
makes.

**The contract structure** decides who employs you, who pays you and who tells
you what work to do. It runs down through the companies: client, prime
contractor, subcontractor, sub-subcontractor. Your instructions come down this
line and nowhere else.

**The site safety structure** decides who is responsible for everybody being
safe in one shared place. It is set by law, it cuts across all the companies at
once, and it gives certain people authority over your movements — where you may
go, when an area is closed, when work stops — regardless of who employs them.

A person can therefore have real authority over you without being your boss.

## Why it matters on the job

Because on your first morning somebody in a different-coloured helmet will tell
you to do something, and you need to know which of two answers is correct.

If they are telling you to **stop, move, leave an area, or put something on**,
that is site safety authority and you comply immediately. Then you tell your
supervisor.

If they are telling you to **do work** — insulate that, move this material, come
and help with a lift — the answer is that instructions about work come through
your own company. The right response is not refusal and not compliance. It is to say
that you will ask your supervisor, and then actually ask. See
[24.2.03](../ch24/24.2.03-how-to-ask-without-guessing.md).

New workers get this wrong in both directions. Some treat every instruction from
a stranger as binding, and end up doing another company's work with their own
company carrying the risk. Some treat safety instructions as negotiable because
the person is not their supervisor. Both are avoidable by knowing the two
structures exist.

## In detail

### Reading the chart on your first morning

There is a document that answers the question of who your company works under
on this site, and it is normally displayed where everyone can see it.

Where a prime contractor subcontracts work above a value set in the 建設業法, it
must compile a 施工体制台帳 listing every company on the job and what each of
them is doing, and must post a 施工体系図 — a tree diagram showing how those
companies sit under one another. On public work the requirement bites as soon as
there is any subcontracting at all, whatever the value.

Find that chart. Find your company's name on it. Trace the line upwards. In five
minutes you will know something that otherwise takes weeks to learn by
inference: whether your company is working directly under the prime contractor
or under a mechanical services contractor, and who else is in the same chain.

The same rules require each licensed contractor, including a small
subcontractor, to place a 主任技術者 on the job — a qualified person responsible
for the technical management of that company's work. On a six-person insulation
company, that person may well also be the person standing next to you.

### The safety structure and its thresholds

On a site where several companies work in the same place, one of them carries
statutory duties for the whole. In the wording of the safety law that company is
the 特定元方事業者, and the other companies are 関係請負人.

Where the total number of workers on the site — the prime contractor's own plus
every subcontractor's — reaches fifty, the prime contractor must appoint a
**統括安全衛生責任者** to control safety across the whole site, and in
construction must also appoint a **元方安全衛生管理者** to handle the technical
side of that. The threshold drops to thirty for a few specific kinds of work,
such as tunnelling and certain bridge work. Below fifty there is a lighter
structure rather than none.

Every subcontractor that carries out work itself must then appoint an
**安全衛生責任者** — its own point of contact with that site-wide structure,
usually the same person as your 職長.

Three duties of the prime contractor are worth knowing because you will see all
three happening:

- **A 協議組織 — a joint safety committee of the prime contractor and every subcontractor, meeting regularly.** Your company's 安全衛生責任者 attends it, and decisions from it reach you through your supervisor.
- **Coordination between the companies as work goes on.** This is the machinery through which the pipe crew finishing its testing on level three turns into your instruction for tomorrow.
- **A patrol of the workplace at least once every working day.** Someone from the prime contractor walks your area daily, by law. Expect it and answer their questions.

Number matters here. A building services job with a two-person insulation crew
may have far fewer than fifty people on it, so the full structure above may not
apply on your site — and a hospital or large office project may have several
hundred. Do not assume the shape of one site tells you the shape of the next.

### The paperwork that comes before you do

Before you set foot on a site, your company submits paperwork about you. The
central item is the 作業員名簿, the register of who is coming, along with
evidence of insurance, health checks, and any qualifications the work requires.

This is why turning up unannounced is not how sites work, and why a worker
who turns up without having been submitted is turned away at the gate. It is not
an obstacle put in your way. It is how the prime contractor can state who is on
their site.

Then, on your first day on each new site, you receive 新規入場者教育 — an
induction covering that site: its layout, the hazards, the emergency routes, who
is in charge, what other work is going on around yours.

The legal shape of this is worth getting right, because it is often described
loosely. The duty to train workers sits with **their own employer**. What the
regulations require of the prime contractor on a construction site is to
**support** that briefing — providing a place to hold it and the site
information to hold it with. In practice on a large site the prime contractor
runs the session; on a small building services job your own supervisor may do
it. Either way, the responsibility for you knowing the site is your company's.

The 建設キャリアアップシステム (CCUS) card is a further layer: an industry-wide
card recording who you are, what qualifications you hold and which sites you
have worked on, touched against a reader at the gate on sites that use it.
Whether the sites this employer works on use card readers is not something any
source consulted here settles — it varies a great deal with site size. Ask
before your first day, because getting a card takes time.

### Some things about a small company that are not obvious

**Your supervisor is not always present.** On a two or three person crew working
across several sites, the 職長 may be at another job for part of the day. That
does not transfer their authority to anyone else on the site. See
[01.7.04](../ch01/01.7.04-working-without-a-supervisor-standing-next-to-you.md).

**Not everyone on site is an employee of somebody.** A 一人親方 is a
self-employed tradesman working alone. They appear on the chart as a company in
their own right. They are not junior to you and not senior to you; they are
another contractor.

**A small company is not a low-status one.** The site chart shows tiers of
contract, not tiers of skill. The specialist licence is what puts a six-person
company on a hospital project, and the partner companies working alongside this
employer hold the senior trade certification as a matter of course.

### What is not settled here

Site organisation is one of the areas where practice varies most, and this item
describes the legal frame rather than what any particular site does.

No source consulted here establishes what a normal first morning looks like on a
small building services job in Yamaguchi — how long the induction runs, whether
there is a whole-site morning meeting on a job with fifteen people on it, or
whether a card reader is used. These are exactly the questions to ask before
the first day rather than to work out on it.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking a work instruction from another company.** Stop and move when anyone with site authority tells you to. Take direction about what work to do only from your own company.
- **Not knowing your supervisor's name and phone number on day one.** This is the single most useful piece of information on a site and takes one question to get.
- **Assuming the person in the site office is in charge of you.** They are in charge of the site. Your work comes from your own company's chain.
- **Never looking at the posted chart.** It answers who your company works under, which is otherwise guesswork for weeks. It is posted precisely so that people can read it.
- **Treating the daily patrol or the safety committee as bureaucracy.** They are statutory duties of the prime contractor and the mechanism by which information about other trades reaches you at all.
- **Assuming site rules carry across between sites.** The safety structure depends on how many people are working there, and the induction is site-specific by design. Every new site is a new induction.
- **Turning up without the paperwork submitted.** A day lost at the gate is a day lost for the whole crew, not just for you.
- **Reading contract tier as status.** A second-tier subcontractor with a specialist licence is doing work nobody else on the site is licensed to do.

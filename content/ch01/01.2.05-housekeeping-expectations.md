# Housekeeping expectations

## What it is

Housekeeping is the state a work area is left in when nobody from the crew is
standing in it — the floor it sits on, what is stacked where, whether the
route through it is walkable, whether dust from the day's cutting has settled
on a windowsill someone else has to touch. It is not a single task done at
the end of a job. It is a condition that either exists or does not exist,
hour by hour, in a space that in this trade's case usually belongs to
somebody else.

Three Japanese terms cover different slices of it. 整理整頓 is the ongoing
discipline of keeping things arranged and in order while work is happening —
tools where they belong, offcuts not left underfoot, a pile that has not
crept past where it was agreed to sit. 後片付け is the act of clearing up and
cleaning a specific area on completion of a task, whether that task is the
whole job or just today's stretch of pipe. 清掃 is cleaning itself — removing
the dust, the swarf, the adhesive drips that accumulate around any cutting or
fixing work.

## Why it matters on the job

For a six-person company working across Yamaguchi and western Hiroshima, the
state of a work area is almost the only thing a prime contractor, a ward
manager or a school caretaker actually sees of the company. Nobody watches a
pipe get insulated correctly. Everybody who walks past a taped-off bay at
five o'clock sees whether it is walkable, whether the floor is swept, whether
a stack of glass wool has been left leaning against a wall patients are
wheeled past. [01.1.01](../ch01/01.1.01-general-construction-workflow.md)
already makes the point that 片付け is not the last ten minutes of goodwill
tacked onto a day — this item is about the part of that idea that is a legal
duty as well as a professional one, and about what changes when the space is
not a construction site but a ward, a classroom or an office that reopens the
moment the crew leaves.

That last part is the reason this item exists separately from
[01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md),
[01.2.03](../ch01/01.2.03-access-routes.md) and
[01.2.04](../ch01/01.2.04-tool-and-waste-zones.md), which cover the pieces —
where material sits, how a route stays clear, where waste goes. Housekeeping
is what all three look like added together, at the moment somebody else has
to live in the result.

## In detail

### The legal root: this is a safety duty, not an add-on

労働安全衛生法 (the Act) — the Industrial Safety and Health Act — sets the
statutory root at 第23条: the employer must maintain the passages, floor
surfaces and stairs in the workplaces where it puts people to work, and take
the measures needed for ventilation, daylight, lighting, warmth, moisture
control, rest, evacuation and cleanliness. Cleanliness sits in that list next
to evacuation and lighting, not off to the side of it. Housekeeping is one of
the things the Act treats as part of keeping a workplace safe, not a separate
standard of tidiness layered on top of the safety rules.

Two clauses of the 労働安全衛生規則 (those regulations) sit under that
article and are covered in detail elsewhere in this section rather than
repeated here. 第544条 requires the floor of a work area to be kept free of
tripping and slipping hazards and in a safe state; 第540条 requires passages
to be kept effective at all times. [01.2.03](../ch01/01.2.03-access-routes.md)
covers what those two clauses mean for a route people actually walk along.
What matters here is narrower: those same floor and passage duties do not
switch off once a task is finished for the day. A floor left unswept at five
o'clock is the same floor those clauses govern, whether anyone from the crew
returns to it in the morning or not.

### The clause written for exactly this situation

建設工事公衆災害防止対策要綱 (the outline for preventing harm to third
parties who have nothing to do with the job) has a clause that speaks
directly to work inside an occupied building. 第14 requires the work area
**and its surroundings** to be kept tidy at all times, specifically so that
dust and the like does not become a nuisance to the people nearby. Read it
next to 第1, which sets out what the whole outline exists to prevent: harm to
the life, body and property of third parties, **and nuisance to them**.
Nuisance is written into the definition of the harm, not treated as a milder
concern sitting outside it.

For someone working a ward corridor, that pairing is the single most useful
sentence available in any of the sources behind this item. It says that
annoying the people around the work — the patients, the staff, the pupils —
is itself the thing 第14 exists to prevent, not a courtesy added on top of
the real rules. A pile of dust on a windowsill outside a taped bay is not a
lapse in manners under this reading. It is the specific harm the clause
names. 第16 of the same outline requires a dust source to be damped down or
covered, which is the practical measure that keeps 第14 true rather than
aspirational.

### What the specification actually requires, and what it does not

公共建築工事標準仕様書（機械設備工事編） (the specification) touches
housekeeping in two places, and it is worth being exact about which duty each
one is, because they are not the same size of thing.

1.3.8(3) requires work, through the course of the job, at improving the
working environment and at keeping the site clean and orderly. That clause
covers the daily condition of the site while the job is running — closer in
spirit to 整理整頓 than to a one-off clean.

1.3.11 後片付け is a completion duty. It requires the part of the works
concerned to be cleared up and cleaned once that part is finished. That is a
real clause and a real obligation, but it is narrower than it might sound:
it governs finishing a task, not the daily rhythm of what a corridor looks
like at five o'clock on a day the job continues tomorrow. The daily habit
this item is really about — leaving a ward corridor walkable every evening,
not only on the last day of the job — is trade practice built on top of
1.3.8(3) and on the Act's 第23条, not something 1.3.11 itself requires. Do
not read 1.3.11 as covering more than a completion clean; the sources
consulted here do not support stretching it that far.

One further clause of the regulations is easy to reach for here and easy to
overstate. 第619条 requires daily cleaning of a workplace, a thorough clean
at least once every six months, and a six-monthly pest survey. That clause
sits in the hygiene standards, and it is written for a workplace an employer
runs continuously — an office, a factory floor. Reading it as a rule about a
two-day insulation job inside somebody else's hospital would overstate what
it covers; nothing in it is written with a borrowed space in mind. It is
mentioned here only so a reader who encounters it elsewhere knows what it is
and is not — not as a rule this trade should apply to its own daily
practice.

### Tidy enough to resume, and tidy enough for the building to resume

These are two different standards, and only the second is the one actually
being judged in this trade's work.

Tidy enough for the crew to resume means the work area is safe and usable for
tomorrow's continuation: tools accounted for, a clear route to where cutting
happens, nothing left that could be a hazard to the crew itself when it walks
back in. That is the lower bar, and it is the one a construction site with no
other occupants can get away with meeting.

Tidy enough for the building to resume is a harder standard, and it is the
one that actually applies on a ward, a school or an office. It means the
corridor is walkable by a porter with a bed, the ward is a ward again rather
than a taped-off construction bay, and a ceiling tile that came down to run
pipework is back in its grid — see
[01.5.05](../ch01/01.5.05-ceiling-tiles-and-grid-damage-you-will-be-charged-for.md)
for how that is actually done. A hospital does not stop being a hospital
overnight because a job is in progress inside it, and the space has to be
handed back in a state its own occupants can use that evening, not tomorrow
when the crew returns to continue. [01.4.06](../ch01/01.4.06-leaving-a-space-usable-at-the-end-of-each-day.md)
covers what that handback actually involves at the end of each working day;
this item is about why that standard is the one that matters, not the
mechanics of achieving it.

Meeting only the first standard and calling the day finished is the most
common way this goes wrong. A crew that leaves its own area walkable but
leaves swarf on a windowsill, dust on a bench a nurse will lean on, or a
stack of offcuts against a wall a wheelchair needs to pass has met a
standard nobody set for it and missed the one that was actually being
watched.

### Why this is the visible output of a small company

A six-person subcontractor rarely gets judged on the quality of a joint or
the accuracy of a thickness calculation by anyone outside the trade. The
prime contractor's site manager, the ward sister, the school caretaker — none
of them is equipped to assess whether a pipe was insulated to the right
thermal resistance. What they can assess, every single day, is whether the
space the crew occupied looks like it was occupied by people who were careful
with somebody else's building. That impression compounds. A company whose
areas are consistently walkable and clean gets the benefit of the doubt on
everything else it does; a company whose areas are not gets watched more
closely on everything else, including the things that actually matter more.

### What is not settled here

Whether the crew is expected to eat, take breaks or use toilets inside the
building it is working in, or is expected to use the van or arrange something
separately, is not established by any source consulted here — it depends on
what the site and the building have agreed, and is a question for the
supervisor before the first day of a new job.

Whether a daily sign-off or handover of the space to ward or school staff is
expected — someone from the crew confirming to a named person that the space
is clear before leaving — or whether that handback happens informally with no
record at all, is not stated by any source consulted here.

How a corridor closure gets recorded, or whether it is reopened with any
formal step at all, is a gap [01.2.01](../ch01/01.2.01-restricted-areas.md)
also leaves open, and the two gaps are the same one: nothing consulted for
this section establishes whether any of this is written down anywhere, on a
job this size, or whether it runs entirely on verbal agreement at the morning
meeting.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating housekeeping as a courtesy rather than as part of the Act's 第23条.** Cleanliness sits in the same clause as evacuation and lighting. A messy area is a compliance gap, not a matter of manners.
- **Reading 1.3.11 as covering the daily standard rather than the completion standard.** That clause requires clearing up once a part of the works is finished. It does not, by itself, require the corridor to look presentable every evening of a job that continues the next day — that daily habit rests on 1.3.8(3) and on trade practice, and dressing it in 1.3.11's authority overstates what the clause says.
- **Reaching for 第619条 to justify a daily-cleaning rule on a short job in someone else's building.** That clause is written for a workplace an employer runs continuously. Citing it for a two-day hospital job stretches it past what it covers.
- **Meeting the lower standard and thinking the job is done.** Leaving the area safe for the crew's own return tomorrow is not the same as leaving it usable for the building's occupants tonight. The second is the one actually being judged.
- **Missing that nuisance is inside the outline's own definition of the harm it prevents, not outside it.** 第1 puts nuisance to third parties alongside harm to their life, body and property, and 第14 carries it into a duty to keep the area and its surroundings tidy. Dust settling where somebody else has to touch it is not a soft failure next to the real safety rules.

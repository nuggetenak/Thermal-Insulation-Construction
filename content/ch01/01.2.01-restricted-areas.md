# Restricted areas

## What it is

A restricted area is a place someone has decided you may not enter without a
stated reason, marked in some way, and reversible only by whoever has the
authority to lift it. That sounds simple until you notice how many different
people can create one, for how many different reasons, and how differently
they mark it.

On this trade's sites — corridors in working hospitals, a plant room reached
through a school, an occupied office floor — 立入禁止 (no entry) is not one
rule. It is several separate systems that happen to use the same two words on
a sign: the job's own safety exclusions, the building's permanent restrictions
that exist whether or not any work is happening, and site-wide authority that
overrides both when there is danger. Confusing the three is the mistake this
item exists to prevent.

## Why it matters on the job

The case that actually catches a new worker is not a fenced compound with a
gate. It is a plant room door with a 立入禁止 sign on it that has nothing to
do with the job at all — a permanent notice required by fire-safety law,
aimed at the building's occupants, on the exact door this trade is sent
through every day. Treat every posted restriction as job-related and this
door reads as an instruction to
turn around; treat every posted restriction as safe to ignore because the
last one turned out not to concern this trade, and eventually one that does
concern you gets walked past.

The second reason it matters: a worker's own duty under the
労働安全衛生規則 — the safety and health regulations — at 第585条第2項
is not conditional on being told. It says plainly that people working nearby
must not enter such a place without proper reason. That is a duty on you, not
only on the person who put the sign up.

## In detail

### Who can bar a place, and why

Several different people can restrict entry, for several different reasons,
and the reason decides who can lift it.

**A fall or drop risk on the job itself.** 第530条 of those regulations requires the
employer to prohibit entry to a place with a risk of a person falling, by a
notice or another method, to anyone other than the workers doing the related
work. 第537条 does the same where the work might drop objects onto people
below — catch nets or an exclusion area, or both. These are job-created
restrictions and the contractor doing that work can lift them once the work
and its risk are finished.

**Scaffold and walkway work in progress.** 第564条第1項第2号 requires entry
to be barred to anyone not engaged in the work while a scaffold is being put
up, altered or taken down. 第552条第2項第2号 covers a narrower but common
case: where the handrails or intermediate rails on a temporary walkway
(架設通路) have to come off temporarily because the work needs it, entry by
anyone else must be barred by notice, and 第552条第3項 requires the rails to
go back **immediately** once the need has passed — not at the end of the day,
not when someone remembers.

**Hazardous conditions in the place itself.** 第585条 lists seven categories
where entry is barred with a conspicuous notice: places handling large
quantities of very hot or very cold material; harmful light or ultrasound;
carbon dioxide over 1.5 percent, oxygen under 18 percent, or hydrogen sulfide
over 10 ppm; places giving off harmful gas, vapor or dust; places handling
harmful substances; and places with a marked risk of pathogen contamination.
Of these, the ones this trade actually meets are plant rooms handling hot or
cold equipment, spaces with harmful dust, and oxygen-deficient spaces. The
last of those is confined-space territory — see
[01.5.06](../ch01/01.5.06-when-a-space-counts-as-confined-and-the-rules-change.md)
rather than treating it as settled here.

**The building itself, independent of any job.** A hospital ward, an
operating suite, a server room, an occupied classroom, an examination room —
these are restricted by the building's own function, not by anything a
contractor did. Nobody working on the insulation package has the authority to
lift that kind of restriction, because nobody working on it created it. Entry
into these is governed by whatever arrangement the building has made with the
job, covered for hospitals in
[01.4.02](../ch01/01.4.02-hospitals-noise-dust-infection-control-restricted-hours.md)
and for occupied buildings generally in
[01.4.01](../ch01/01.4.01-why-occupied-building-work-is-different-from-a-construction-.md).

### The sign that means two different things

Spec clause 1.7.4(1) requires signs mandated by fire law — dangerous-goods
notices, no-entry notices at plant room entrances, 火気厳禁 (no naked
flames) notices — to be installed as part of the finished works. These are
permanent fixtures of the building, not temporary job restrictions, and they
do not go away when the job finishes. A plant room can carry a 立入禁止 sign
aimed at keeping building occupants out of a mechanical space, while being
precisely where this trade is sent to insulate the pipework inside it.

The practical answer is not to treat the sign as irrelevant to you, because a
sign restricting general entry to a hazardous plant room is also telling you
something true about the room. The practical answer is that a worker's
company has arranged access for the job, and that arrangement — not the sign
by itself — is what tells you the restriction does not apply to you today.
If nobody has told a worker that arrangement exists, that is a question for
the supervisor, not an assumption to make standing at the door.

### 作業場, 仮囲い, and where a large-site picture stops applying

Large new-build sites make this visible in one glaring, physical form: a
仮囲い, a temporary hoarding around the whole site, required by
公衆災害防止対策要綱 第21 to stand at least 1.8 m high (3 m where
particularly necessary), strong enough not to blow over, with gates kept
closed except when the work needs them open and a notice prohibiting public
entry. Inside that boundary is the 作業場, the work area, and everyone
outside it is the public the hoarding exists to protect.

That picture does not survive the move into an occupied building, and it is
worth saying plainly rather than leaving the reader to guess. There is no
1.8 m hoarding around a taped-off length of hospital corridor. The boundary
is often barrier tape, a folding sign, or nothing more than a verbal
agreement with the ward that this stretch is out of use today, and the
person on the other side of it is a patient on a trolley rather than a
pedestrian on a pavement. The legal duty behind 仮囲い — keep the work
separated from people who are not part of it — still applies. What changes
is that nothing about the building supplies a fence to do it, and the crew's
own set-up is what makes that separation real or fails to.

### What is not settled here

Whether a given site issues any written record when a corridor is closed for
the day, or whether it is only announced verbally at the morning meeting, is
not established by any source consulted here — ask the supervisor on
arrival. Hospital infection-control zoning — who draws the boundary inside a
ward, what marks it, and what the crew is actually told before crossing it —
is real and site-specific, and belongs to
[01.4.02](../ch01/01.4.02-hospitals-noise-dust-infection-control-restricted-hours.md)
rather than to this item. Which parts of a school are out of bounds during
term time is likewise not settled here.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading a permanent building sign as a temporary job restriction, or the reverse.** A 立入禁止 notice at a plant room door under spec 1.7.4(1) is often a fire-safety fixture aimed at the public, not a statement about whether the job may enter. Confirm access through the supervisor rather than guessing from the sign alone.
- **Assuming a fallen handrail or a missing scaffold panel is a temporary convenience rather than a barred area.** 第552条第2項第2号 requires that gap to be treated as no-entry the moment the rails come off, for anyone not doing that specific work.
- **Treating 第585条第2項 as somebody else's rule.** The duty not to enter a hazardous place without proper reason is written on the worker, not only on the employer who posted the notice.
- **Expecting a fenced compound and finding none.** On a hospital corridor or a school plant room there is usually no hoarding at all. The absence of a physical barrier does not mean the area is not restricted — it means the restriction is carried some other way, and it is worth finding out what that way is before assuming free access.
- **Lifting a restriction that was never yours to lift.** A fall-risk exclusion created by this trade's own work can be reopened once that work and its risk are finished. A ward restriction created by the hospital cannot — that decision belongs to the building, not to the job.

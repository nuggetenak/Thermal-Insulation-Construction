# Tool and waste zones

## What it is

Two separate questions, both about where things sit while they are not being
used. Where does a tool bag, a cordless drill, a box of blades live during
the working day, without becoming something someone else trips over or gets
cut on. And where does everything the work throws off — offcuts, packaging,
swarf, used blades, spent adhesive tubes — go, both while it waits and once
it leaves the building.

On a large new-build site both questions have obvious physical answers: a
tool store in a site cabin, a skip in the compound. On this trade's actual
jobs — a corridor in a working hospital, a plant room reached through a
school, an occupied office over a weekend — there is usually no cabin and no
skip. The tools live in whatever bag came off the van, in the space the crew
is working in. The waste goes into a sack, and that sack sits somewhere in
the building until someone carries it out. Neither of those defaults is
wrong. Both need a decision behind them rather than an assumption.

## Why it matters on the job

The tool half is an immediate hazard: something on a floor or a passage that
someone else — a patient on a trolley, a porter, a cleaner, another trade —
walks into, trips on, or is cut by. The two clauses this rests on, covered
below, are not abstractions. They are the reason a bag left in the middle of
a ward corridor is a genuine breach, not a tidiness complaint.

The waste half is a different kind of question, and it is the one worth
taking seriously before assuming an answer. Whether the offcuts from a day's
work can simply go back in the van is not a matter of what is convenient. It
is a legal test, set out in the Act governing waste disposal, and the test
turns on facts about the job — its contract value, how the waste is
segregated, where it is going — not on what fits in the vehicle. Getting
this wrong is not a paperwork slip; it is carrying regulated waste without
the authority to do so.

## In detail

### Tools: the floor and passage duties they actually breach

[01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md) covers the staging pile — a
stack of material against a corridor wall — and the 1.8 m clearance above an
indoor passage that piled material is not allowed to breach. The tool angle
is different but sits in the same part of the 労働安全衛生規則, the
regulations governing workplace safety generally.

第544条 requires the floor of a work area to be kept free of tripping and
slipping hazards and kept in a safe state generally. A tool bag left open on
a ward floor, a coil of cable across a doorway, a box of fixings tipped out
and not gathered back up — all of these are exactly what that clause is
written against, and it applies to the floor a worker is standing on to do
the work, not only to the corridor outside it.

第542条 covers the indoor passage itself: its width has to suit how it is
actually used, its surface has to be kept free of tripping, slipping and
punch-through hazards, and nothing may be placed within 1.8 m above it.
Where [01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md) applies that clause to
a pile of pipe sections, the same clause applies to a tool bag set down in
the passage rather than inside the work area itself. A passage a cleaner's
trolley or a hospital bed needs to pass through is not a place to leave
anything, tools included, even for a few minutes between tasks.

### Fire: keeping the use of fire to what the work needs

建設工事公衆災害防止対策要綱 第18 sets out how fire is meant to be handled on
a job. Where the law requires notifying the fire station in advance of using
fire, that notification is given. Beyond that: the use of fire is kept to
the minimum directly needed for the work itself; anything else needing fire
is done in a designated place chosen because it carries no fire risk, and
nowhere else; extinguishers and firefighting equipment are kept matched to
the scale of the work; fire is not used near easily ignited material; and
where welding, cutting or similar work may throw sparks, a watcher is posted
as necessary and measures are taken to limit how far the sparks can travel.

This trade's own version of fire work is smaller than welding but not nothing —
a heat gun for shrink-wrap, an angle grinder throwing sparks off a metal
jacketing edge. Making the joints in the pipe itself is the pipe installer's
work rather than this trade's, so a soldering iron on a copper joint is that
crew's hot work and not yours; see
[01.1.02](../ch01/01.1.02-mechanical-equipment-trades-overview.md). The clause
does not distinguish by scale; it asks the same questions of a heat gun near a
box of packaging as it would of a welding torch. 危険物, covered by
[01.2.02](../ch01/01.2.02-material-staging.md), and the store marked
火気厳禁 are part of the same picture: adhesives and solvents kept away from
anywhere fire is used, not staged next to it.

### This trade's own failure modes

The two clauses above are general. What actually goes wrong on this trade's
jobs is specific enough to name.

- **Used utility-knife blades dropped into the general waste sack.** A
  cleaner or porter who later lifts that sack by hand has no reason to
  expect a blade in it. A separate container for blades, closed before it
  goes in with anything else, is the difference between a sharp-edge hazard
  and a routine sack of packaging.
- **Jacketing offcuts left with a cut edge facing where someone reaches in.**
  A trimmed strip of metal jacketing has a sharp edge the moment it is cut.
  Left loose in a sack or on a windowsill with that edge exposed, it cuts
  the next hand that goes near it, whether that hand belongs to the crew or
  to someone tidying up after them.
- **Swarf and rivet stems left on a finished hospital floor.** Metal
  fragments from drilling or riveting are small enough to be missed by eye
  and hard enough to mark a vinyl or polished floor the moment someone walks
  or wheels a bed over them. On a floor the building has to keep clean and
  presentable, this is a defect the crew caused after the actual work was
  already finished correctly.
- **Glass wool dust drawn into a return air path.** Cutting or trimming
  glass wool near an open grille or a return duct opening sends dust
  straight into the building's air system, well past the immediate work
  area and past the day the work happened.
- **A cordless tool left on charge overnight in an occupied corridor.** A
  charger plugged into whatever socket was nearest, left running unattended
  in a space the building's own occupants use after the crew has gone, is a
  fire and obstruction risk with nobody present to notice if it goes wrong.

### Waste: who the law says is responsible

This is the question with a real, specific answer, and it is set out in the
廃棄物の処理及び清掃に関する法律, the Act governing waste disposal, called
the Act below. 第21条の3第1項 governs construction work specifically.
Where work runs through successive subcontracts, as it does whenever a job
comes to this trade through a prime contract taken from the client, the
**元請業者 — the contractor that took the job directly from the client — is
treated as the legal generator of the waste the whole job produces.** The
duty to dispose of it properly, the contracts with licensed waste carriers,
and the manifest paperwork that tracks it, belong to that
[元請](../ch01/01.1.01-general-construction-workflow.md), not to the
insulation subcontractor. This is the reason offcuts generally go wherever
the prime contractor's arrangement says they go, rather than wherever is
easiest for the crew that produced them.

第2項 keeps a second half of the duty on the subcontractor's own side: for
the purpose of **storage**, the subcontractor is also treated as a generator
of the waste. How it is held on site — kept segregated, kept out of a
passage, kept away from an ignition source — is this trade's own
responsibility, whatever the prime contractor's arrangement for eventual
disposal turns out to be. Stating only the first half of this and not the
second leaves the false impression that the subcontractor has no
responsibility for waste at all once it decides who takes it away, which is
not what the Act says.

第3項, read with 施行規則第18条の2, is the narrow route by which a
subcontractor may lawfully carry construction waste itself, rather than
leaving it to the prime contractor's arrangement. It is not the only lawful
way waste moves. Under the Act's ordinary scheme the generator commissions a
licensed industrial waste carrier — 第12条第5項 — and a company that holds
that licence can be the carrier commissioned to do it. The route below is the
one for a subcontractor carrying the waste as its own, without a licence and
without a commission, and its conditions are what that route costs. It
requires a **written
term in the subcontract** allowing it, and then every one of the following
to hold at once: the work is not demolition, and not new build or extension
work; its contract value is 5,000,000 yen or less — or, for work remedying a
defect in a building already handed over to the client, the same value
limit applies; each load carried is 1 cubic metre or less, kept segregated
by type so that this is evident on sight; the destination is a facility —
a transfer station or a storage site both count — in the **same prefecture
as the site, or a prefecture adjacent to it**, and one the prime contractor
owns or otherwise has the right to use; and the waste is not stored anywhere
along the route. For a company based in Iwakuni running day trips across
Yamaguchi and into western Hiroshima, the adjacent-prefecture limit is not a
technicality to skim past — it is a live geographic question about which
loads that route can lawfully cover, and it is worth asking rather than
assuming a familiar drive counts.

What the storage standards under 第12条第2項 actually require — how a bin or
sack is kept, what marking it needs, how long it can sit — is not carried
here. It is set by cabinet order and ministerial ordinance beyond what this
item covers, and in practice it is the prime contractor's arrangement for
the site. Ask what that arrangement is rather than guessing at it.

### What the specification adds

Clause 1.3.9 of the specification governing public building work requires
発生材, the material a job generates as arisings rather than brings to
site, to be reduced, reused and recycled where possible. Whatever can be
recycled is **分別** — segregated by type — and taken to the facility
designated for it, with a record kept. Everything else leaves the site and
is disposed of under the laws that apply, including the Act above, and that
disposal is reported. Clause 1.3.8(2) requires safety data sheets for
paints, adhesives and similar chemical products to be kept to hand, and
requires the information about the chemicals actually being handled to be
**displayed in the work area itself**, by labels or similar — not filed away
somewhere else on site. Clause 1.3.8(3) sets a general duty to keep working
at the working environment and at keeping the site clean and orderly for the
length of the job, which is the specification's own version of the same
instinct behind 第544条 above.

Segregation and disposal in detail belong to
[08.6.06](../ch08/08.6.06-waste-segregation.md); tagging material that
turns out defective belongs to
[07.6.05](../ch07/07.6.05-defect-tagging.md); day-to-day housekeeping of the
work area belongs to
[01.2.05](../ch01/01.2.05-housekeeping-expectations.md); offcuts of insulation
material and metal waste specifically belong to
[22.5.01](../ch22/22.5.01-offcuts.md) and
[22.5.03](../ch22/22.5.03-metal-waste.md).

### What is not settled here

Whether a written hot-work permit is issued on a given site before cutting,
soldering or grinding that could throw sparks, and what that permit looks
like on this employer's jobs, is not stated by any source consulted here —
ask the supervisor before assuming either way. Whether this company's
offcuts in practice go back to the yard in the van or into the prime
contractor's own skip is not established here either; the law above is what
makes that a real question with a defined answer rather than a matter of
convenience, but which of the two actually happens on a given job depends on
the contract in place and is worth confirming rather than guessing. And
where a filled waste sack may be staged inside an occupied building while it
waits to be carried out — a corner of the plant room, a corridor recess, a
spot a ward has agreed to — is not answered by anything read for this item.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving a tool bag or loose tools in a passage rather than inside the work area.** 第542条 applies to the passage itself, not only to material staged there, and a passage a bed or a trolley needs is not a place to set anything down between tasks.
- **Dropping used utility-knife blades into the general waste sack.** Whoever lifts that sack next has no reason to expect a blade in it. Keep blades in their own closed container before they go anywhere near the rest of the waste.
- **Leaving jacketing offcuts with the cut edge exposed.** A trimmed edge is sharp the moment it is cut, and it stays that way sitting in a sack or on a sill until someone's hand meets it.
- **Assuming offcuts can go back in the van because it is easiest.** 第21条の3第1項 makes the prime contractor the legal generator of the job's waste in the ordinary case. Carrying it yourself lawfully needs the specific written contract term and every condition under 施行規則第18条の2, not just a spare seat in the van.
- **Treating the same-or-adjacent-prefecture limit as a rough guide.** For a crew running day trips out of Iwakuni into Hiroshima, that clause is the actual test for whether a load can be carried under the subcontractor route at all, and it is worth checking rather than assuming a familiar drive qualifies.
- **Stating only the disposal half of the waste duty and not the storage half.** 第21条の3第2項 keeps the subcontractor responsible for how waste is held on site regardless of who eventually disposes of it. Sorting and segregation on site remain this trade's own job even when the skip belongs to someone else.

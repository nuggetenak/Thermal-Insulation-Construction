# Mobilization

## What it is

Mobilization is everything that has to happen before a crew can start
insulating pipework on a site they have never stood on. For a large new-build
contractor it can mean a site compound going up — a hoarding, a site office,
welfare facilities. For a six-person insulation company working van-based day
trips across Yamaguchi and western Hiroshima, none of that belongs to this
trade. There is no compound. There is a van loaded the evening before, a
drive the next morning, and a first arrival at a building somebody else
already owns and runs.

What does not shrink to fit a small crew is the paperwork. A worker who has
not been registered with the site cannot walk through the gate, and the
documents that authorize the job to begin at all have to exist before anyone
picks up a tool. Mobilization is the point where three separate things —
who is allowed on site, what has been agreed can be built and how, and where
the crew's own company stands in the chain running the job — all have to be
in place at once, for the first time, on unfamiliar ground.

## Why it matters on the job

The reason this deserves its own item rather than folding into
[01.3.02](../ch01/01.3.02-preparation.md) is that mobilization failures cost
the whole crew, not one person. A worker whose paperwork was never submitted
does not lose a morning quietly catching up later — the gate turns them away,
and if the crew is small, that is one pair of hands short for the whole job
that day. A job that starts without the documents governing how the work is
to be done is not simply informal; it is a job proceeding on the basis of
nothing that has been agreed, which is exactly what the specification's
sequencing exists to prevent.

The other reason it matters is less obvious and more personal. A new worker
arriving on a first site reasonably assumes the programme, the plan and the
sequence simply arrive as instructions from above, with nothing the crew's
own company had any say in. That is not the whole picture. The company a
worker works for has a standing right to be consulted before certain
decisions are fixed, and knowing that right exists — even if using it is not
a new worker's job — changes how the first morning reads. It is the
difference between being handed a plan and understanding that the plan was
supposed to have been discussed with someone.

## In detail

### The paperwork that precedes the crew

Before anyone from this trade sets foot on a new site, their name has to be
on it. [01.1.04](../ch01/01.1.04-subcontractor-and-site-organization-basics.md)
covers 作業員名簿 and 新規入場者教育 and the wider site safety structure they
sit inside — that is where the mechanics live. What belongs here is the
sequencing consequence, because it is the part that actually bites a small
crew: submission has a lead time, and a worker added the night before a job
starts may simply not be processed in time. One person turned away at the
gate on a six-person job is not a one-person problem. The whole crew's output
for that day drops by however much that person would have done, and the work
does not wait for the paperwork to catch up.

### The documents that have to exist before work starts

The standard specification for mechanical services work on public building
projects — cited here from its Part 1, the general common provisions that
sit ahead of the trade-specific parts, since the specification reuses clause
numbers across those later parts and a bare clause number on its own
identifies nothing — sets out what has to be produced, and when, before
work can begin.

Clause 1.2.2(1) requires an overall plan, the 総合施工計画書, covering the
works as a whole, produced before starting and submitted to the 監督職員, the
client's supervising officer on public work. Clause 1.2.2(3) then requires a
narrower document for each type of work — a 工種別施工計画書 setting out the
quality plan, how that specific work will be carried out, and what will be
confirmed at which stage — produced and submitted before that particular
work starts, not before the job as a whole starts. That clause carries a
proviso: it does not apply where the 監督職員 has given consent in advance.
Clause 1.2.2(4) adds that the parts of both documents dealing with the
quality plan need the 監督職員's formal consent, and so does any later change
to them. This account of 1.2.2 is not a complete one — the specification
carries further paragraphs this item does not cover, and anyone relying on
the clause directly should read it in full rather than from this summary.

Clause 1.2.3(1) puts the 施工図 — the drawing showing how a detail is
actually to be built on this job — on the same footing: produced before the work they
cover, needing the 監督職員's consent, subject to the same advance-consent
proviso, and coordinated with related works so that a later change does not
disrupt what is already agreed.

The single sentence that ties all of this together is clause 1.5.1: the work
is carried out on the basis of the design documents, the 実施工程表, the
施工計画書 and the 施工図. That is the spine of the whole
sequence — the paper decides what gets built, and it has to exist before
着手, the start of work, not after.

The 実施工程表 has a clause of its own, and it is stricter than the word
"programme" suggests. Clause 1.2.1 requires it to be produced before 着手 and
**approved by the 監督職員**; where a change to it becomes necessary through a
change in the contract conditions, it is changed at once and goes back for
approval before the affected work starts, and other changes are reported. So
this is not a working document that drifts as the job moves — it is a
controlled one that needs consent to move. The documents closest to "what the
crew is actually working to this week" are the weekly, monthly and per-trade
programmes at 1.2.1(5), which are supplements produced when the 監督職員 asks
for them. It is worth holding that distinction against the 工程表 that
[01.1.01](../ch01/01.1.01-general-construction-workflow.md) covers.

None of this paper is something a six-person insulation company is likely to
be producing itself. It sits with the 元請負人 — and that word is worth
getting right, because the Act does not mean what the English "prime
contractor" suggests. 建設業法第2条第5項 defines the 元請負人 as **the party
that orders work under a subcontract**, provided it holds a licence. It is
whoever engaged your company, at whatever tier. On a job this size that is
usually the mechanical services contractor above the insulation
subcontractor in the chain. What a worker on the ground needs to know is not
how to write a 施工計画書 but that one has to exist, that its quality-plan
content needed formal consent before the work started, and that if a task
seems to be proceeding without any plan behind it at all, that is worth
raising rather than assuming is normal.

### A right the crew's company has

建設業法第24条の2 places a duty on the 元請負人 — read as above, the company
that engaged yours, not necessarily the main contractor on the site: before it fixes the detailed
construction programme, the specific working methods, or the other matters
that are for the prime contractor to decide, it must hear the opinion of the
subcontractor **in advance**. This is a flat legal obligation on the prime
contractor, not a courtesy it may or may not extend.

What follows from that duty is a right the crew's employer holds, not a
right an individual worker can invoke directly on site. Nothing in the Act
requires the prime contractor to consult each worker, or even to consult
through a channel a new hire would ever see. What it does mean is that the
programme a worker is handed on a first morning is not supposed to have
arrived unilaterally — the company that employs them was entitled to be
asked about the working methods before they were fixed. Whether that
consultation genuinely happened, and how, is not something a new worker on
site can check, and it is not their job to police it. It is worth knowing
the right exists, because it explains why a supervisor or the company owner
may push back on a proposed method or date rather than simply accepting it,
and because a worker who later runs a crew of their own will be the one this
duty is owed to.

### The van, loaded the night before

For this employer's shape of work — projects averaging about two months,
occupied buildings as often as new build, day trips rather than a site
office — mobilization has a physical form that none of the specification
clauses above describe, because none of them were written with a van-based
crew in mind.

Materials, tools and access equipment that are known in advance get loaded
the evening before, so the morning is a drive rather than a scramble.
Anything bought on the way — consumables running low, an item discovered
missing only once the previous day's work exposed the gap — is a second-best
option rather than the plan, because a shop on the route may not have what
is needed and the detour itself eats into a fixed day. What exactly gets
loaded the night before versus picked up on the way, and how that decision
gets made, is not something any source consulted here sets out, and it is a
reasonable question to ask a supervisor directly rather than to guess.

Arriving at a building whose own staff have no particular reason to know a
job is starting that day is routine on this kind of work, especially in
occupied hospitals, schools and offices. The crew reports to whoever the job
has arranged as the point of contact on arrival — typically someone from the
mechanical contractor or the site's own facilities staff, depending on the
job — and if that person is not there, the sensible response is to wait and
confirm rather than to start working on the assumption that yesterday's
understanding still holds today. A first morning on a new site is not a
morning where guessing is a reasonable substitute for confirming.

Section 01.1 already covers how much of a working day is 段取り, the
setting-up that makes the work itself possible, rather than the work
itself — see [01.1.01](../ch01/01.1.01-general-construction-workflow.md). On
a first morning at a new site that share is larger than usual: finding the
right floor, finding who is in charge locally, working out where materials
can be staged without blocking a corridor someone else needs, and confirming
which parts of the building are and are not available before a tool is
picked up. None of that is time lost. It is the mobilization the paperwork
above was written to make possible.

### What is not settled here

What mobilization actually looks like for this employer on a two-month job —
how much genuinely gets done the evening before against how much happens on
the first morning, and who decides — is not established by any source
consulted here. That is a question for the crew's own supervisor or the
company owner, not something a specification clause answers.

Whether this company's crews ever produce a 工種別施工計画書 themselves, or
whether the mechanical contractor above them writes it and the crew simply
works to what it says, is likewise unsettled here. On a job this size the
second is the more plausible arrangement, but no source consulted confirms
it either way — ask the supervisor who actually holds that document on a
given job.

Who signs off a completed stage of the work on an insulation package this
small, and whether the 監督職員's formal consent to that person is a real,
visible event on a job of this scale, is also not addressed by anything
consulted here.

### One limit on everything above

The specification quoted throughout this item governs public building work.
Private jobs usually follow it, and the employer these readers are joining
works on government and hospital buildings among others. But usually is not
always, and the specification that applies to a given job is the one that job
is working to. Confirm with your supervisor which document your site follows
before treating any clause here as binding on it. The 建設業法 duties are
different in kind: they apply to the contract itself, whoever the client is.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a missed submission deadline as one worker's problem.** A worker added to a job the night before it starts may not clear the gate the next morning, and on a six-person crew that is a day's output lost for everyone, not a personal inconvenience. Ask early, not the night before.
- **Assuming the programme simply arrived from nowhere.** 建設業法第24条の2 requires the prime contractor to hear the subcontractor's opinion in advance of fixing the working methods and the programme. That right belongs to the company, not to an individual worker, but a plan handed down was supposed to have already involved someone from this side of the job.
- **Expecting a compound and finding none.** There is no site office and no hoarding waiting on a two-month job in an occupied building. The absence of that physical setup does not mean nothing had to be arranged first — it means the arranging happened as paperwork and phone calls rather than as construction.
- **Starting work on the assumption that yesterday's contact and arrangement still hold.** If the person the crew is meant to report to is not there on arrival, confirm before starting rather than proceeding on memory of how it worked last time.
- **Reading 段取り on a first morning as time not yet spent working.** Finding the floor, the local contact and a place to stage material safely is the mobilization the documents above were written to make possible. It is real work, and it is usually a larger share of the first morning than any other day on the job.

# Mechanical/equipment trades overview

## What it is

A building is put up by two broadly separate groups of trades.

**建築** is the building itself: the structure, the walls, the floors, the
ceilings, the finishes. **設備** is everything that runs through and inside it:
pipes, ducts, cables, plant, sprinklers, lifts. Insulation work is a 設備 trade,
and the people whose work you touch every day are almost all 設備 people.

Inside 設備, the work splits again into a small number of licensed trades. The
split is not just custom. It is written into the 建設業法, which licenses
construction companies by trade category, and it decides who is allowed to do
what.

## Why it matters on the job

Three practical reasons.

The first is that you have to know who to ask. A first-year helper spends a lot
of the first months asking questions, and asking the right company saves
everyone's time. The person who knows whether that pipe has been tested is the
pipe installer. The person who knows whether that cable is live is the
electrician. Neither of them is your supervisor.

The second is that these boundaries are also boundaries on what you may touch.
Your company holds a licence for insulation work. It does not hold one for
electrical work or for fire protection work. Moving a sprinkler head so you can
get a pipe section on is not a favour and not initiative — it is another
company's licensed work, and it is the kind of thing that gets a company removed
from a site.

The third is that the trade names are how instructions are phrased. When someone
says the 保温屋 are on the fourth floor, they mean you.

## In detail

### The licensed categories that matter to you

The 建設業法 divides construction into a fixed list of trade categories, and a
company needs a licence in a category to take on work of that kind above a
minimum contract value. Four of those categories describe almost everybody you
will meet in a ceiling void.

| Category | Covers | On site they are called |
|---|---|---|
| 管工事 | Heating, cooling, refrigeration, air conditioning, water supply, drainage, sanitary work, gas piping and duct installation | 設備屋, 配管屋, ダクト屋 |
| 電気工事 | Power generation, distribution, and the electrical installations inside a building, including lighting | 電気屋 |
| 消防施設工事 | Fire alarm systems, extinguishing systems including sprinklers and hydrants, escape equipment | 消防設備屋 |
| 熱絶縁工事業 | Thermally insulating a structure or the equipment in it | 保温屋 |

Two things in that table are worth pausing on.

**Duct installation is 管工事, not a category of its own.** The company that
hangs the ductwork is licensed under the same category as the company that runs
the pipework, and is often the same company. Insulating that ductwork is your
work, not theirs. See chapter 37 for the ductwork craft itself.

**熱絶縁工事業 is a licence in its own right.** Insulation is not a sub-part of
pipework licensing. The official description of the category is short and broad:
thermally insulating a structure or the equipment in it, with examples given
that run from air conditioning and refrigeration plant through to power and
process plant, and including sprayed urethane. That breadth is why the same
licence covers a hospital chilled water riser and a factory steam line.

The employer these readers are joining holds that licence. A company of six
people holding a specialist licence is completely normal in this industry, and
the licence — not the size — is what lets it be on the site.

### The people, and what they are actually doing

Names on a table are less useful than knowing what the person in front of you is
doing and why it affects you.

**The pipe installer (配管).** Hangs supports, runs pipe, makes joints, fits
valves and strainers, and then tests the system. Their work has to be complete
and proved before yours starts, and the interface is not negotiable. See
[01.1.03](../ch01/01.1.03-relationship-between-piping-hvac-electrical-fire-protection-.md).

**The duct installer.** Hangs and joins ductwork, fits dampers and
diffusers. Ductwork is large, light and easy to deform. Standing on a duct, or
using one as a handhold, damages someone else's work and is visible afterwards.

**The electrician.** Cable tray, conduit, containment, lighting, and the
power and control wiring to mechanical plant. There is a special case worth
knowing from the start: some pipework carries electric trace heating, which is
an electrical installation lying directly under your insulation. Never insulate
over trace heating without being told that it has been tested and released.

**The fire protection installer.** Sprinkler pipework, heads, alarm
devices, fire dampers. Their equipment has clearances and access requirements
that insulation can breach without anyone noticing until an inspection. See
[01.6.04](../ch01/01.6.04-damage-you-can-cause-to-ductwork-cabling-and-sprinkler-pipe.md).

**The building trades.** Ceiling grid installers, dry lining, painters. These
are the people who close the space over your work, and the people whose finished
surfaces you can damage while carrying a three-metre length of jacketing through
a corridor.

**The prime contractor's own staff.** Not usually installing anything. Managing
the programme, the safety structure and the interfaces between everyone above.
See [01.1.04](../ch01/01.1.04-subcontractor-and-site-organization-basics.md).

### 空調設備 and 衛生設備

Within 管工事 there is a further split that you will hear constantly, because
the two halves are often different crews and sometimes different companies.

**空調設備** is air conditioning and ventilation: chilled and hot water pipework
to air handling units and fan coil units, refrigerant pipework, and ductwork.
Most of the insulation on a building services job is on this half of the work,
and most of the condensation control problems are too.

**衛生設備** is water supply, hot water, drainage and sanitary fittings.
Insulation here is more often about stopping condensation on cold water pipework
and preventing heat loss from hot water pipework, and the runs are usually
smaller in diameter.

Which half you are working on changes what materials come off the van and what
the failure mode is if you get it wrong. Chapter 41 covers the systems
themselves.

### Where the boundary is genuinely unclear

Trade boundaries are cleaner on paper than on site, and two cases come up often
enough to name.

The first is fire-stopping at a penetration. Where a pipe passes through a fire
compartment, the sealing of the hole is a fire protection matter, the pipe is
the pipe installer's, and the insulation running through the opening is yours.
The standard specification for public building services work is specific that
where a chilled water pipe passes through a fire compartment, the insulation at
that penetration is rock wool — so the material at that one point is dictated by
a requirement that has nothing to do with thermal performance. Who physically
does what at that opening varies by site.

The second is who repairs damage to insulation caused by a later trade. The
damage is real, the cost is real, and no source consulted here settles who pays
as a general rule; it is decided job by job. Report damage when you find it
rather than quietly making it good, so that the record shows what happened. See
[01.6.03](../ch01/01.6.03-damage-caused-to-your-work-by-trades-following-you.md).

## Common mistakes

- **Doing another trade's work to save time.** Moving a sprinkler head, unclipping a cable, loosening a duct hanger. Each of those is licensed work belonging to someone else, and doing it creates a liability your supervisor then has to explain.
- **Assuming the person in the ceiling void next to you works for the same company.** On a busy services floor there may be four companies within five metres. Ask before you move anything that is not yours.
- **Treating ductwork as a walkway or a handhold.** It carries its own weight and very little else. Deformed ductwork is visible, expensive and traceable to whoever was above it.
- **Insulating over electric trace heating that has not been released.** The heating cable is an electrical installation, it has to be tested, and once your insulation and jacketing are on, testing it means taking them off again.
- **Using trade nicknames in a formal report.** 保温屋 and 電気屋 are what people say on site. The company name and the licensed trade are what belongs in writing.
- **Believing the licence categories describe skill levels.** They describe scope, not seniority. A specialist licence held by a six-person company is the normal structure of this industry, not a sign of a small operation being kept away from real work.
- **Assuming the split between 空調設備 and 衛生設備 is the same everywhere.** On a small job one crew often does both. Which crew is which on your site is a question for your supervisor, not something to infer from the drawings.

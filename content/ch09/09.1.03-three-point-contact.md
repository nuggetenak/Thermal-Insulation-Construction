# Three-point contact

## What it is

Three-point contact (三点支持) is a rule about **moving**, not about working. You
have four points of contact with a ladder: two hands and two feet. While you are
going up or down, three of them stay on the ladder and only one moves at a time.

That is the whole rule, and it is not something invented for this page. Japan's
national occupational safety research institute states it in exactly those
terms — of the four points that are your hands and feet, keep three connected to
the ladder while you climb. The construction industry's safety association
prints it on its current ladder leaflet as a rule: when you go up or down, you
always do it with three-point contact.

Both sources state it for ladders. The portable leaning ladder is 移動はしご in
the regulations, and it is the one these figures are about. Its parts have names
you will hear on site: the rungs you stand on are 踏桟, and the two long side
rails are 支柱. Which of those two your hands should be gripping is not
stated in any source consulted here — ask your supervisor which your company
teaches, and do that.

## Why it matters on the job

Because climbing is when ladders kill people, and almost nobody expects that.

The institute analysed ten years of fatal falls (墜落) from ladders in Japanese
construction. 103 workers died. **52 of them — just over half — were climbing up
or down at the moment they fell.** 29 were working from the ladder. For 22 the
record does not say which.

That is backwards from the intuition. You would assume the dangerous part is
being up there with a knife in your hand. The figures say the dangerous part is
the trip.

It gets more specific than that. Those 103 deaths break down by type of work
into three groups — civil engineering, building work, and 設備工事, building
services and equipment work. 設備工事 accounts for 35 of them. Not roofing. Not
civil engineering.

Insulation work is normally counted inside that third group, which is what makes
those 35 deaths the closest figure in the study to your own job. Be exact about
what that means, though: the study stops at the three groups and does not break
them down by trade, so no source consulted here counts insulation workers
separately. 設備工事 also covers the pipefitters, ductwork installers and
electricians you work alongside.

## In detail

### Why three, and how it breaks

Four points. The rule asks for three. Climbing one rung means moving one point at
a time, so three stay put. Four minus one is three, and the arithmetic comes out
exactly even.

Now put something in one hand. You start the climb with three points instead of
four. Move a foot and you are on two. Every step of that climb is a two-point
step, and two points is how people fall.

This is why both sources tell you not to climb holding things — though not with
the same force, and the difference is worth knowing.

The association's leaflet prints it as a flat rule on its ladder page, with no
exception attached: do not go up or down holding objects. The institute is more
conditional. Its analysis lists carrying a *heavy* load in one hand among the
unsafe behaviours found in the fatal cases, and its countermeasure is worded
against loads that stop you keeping your body stable, rather than against
carrying anything at all.

Take the flat version. It is the one that needs no judgement from someone in
their first year, and judging what counts as a stable load is exactly the
judgement you do not yet have.

Insulation work makes this hard in a particular way. A roll of blanket, a
preformed section, a coil of banding, a box of pins — none of it is heavy, and
that is the trap. Light material makes carrying it up feel reasonable. The
arithmetic does not care what it weighs. **A hand holding something is not a
point of contact.**

What you do instead depends on the site: a rope and a bucket, a second person
passing material up, a materials hoist, or simply climbing empty and having it
handed to you. Ask your supervisor what your crew does before your first climb.
It is a normal question and every site has an answer.

### Coming down

Turning your back to the ladder on the way down is the other behaviour to avoid.
Face outwards and your hands have nothing to hold. You are on two points at
best, on the leg of the trip where you are tired and can see the rungs least.

**Attribution matters here, so be exact.** The institute states this for ladders
directly: do not go up or down with your back to the ladder. The association's
leaflet also prohibits it, but prints it among its 脚立 rules rather than its
ladder rules. So for a leaning ladder the rule rests on the institute alone —
one source, not two — and for a stepladder it rests on the leaflet.

Either way, go down facing the equipment, the same way you went up.

### What three-point contact does not do

It keeps you attached to the ladder while you move. That is all it does, and
three limits follow from it.

**It does not make a ladder a place to work from.** The association's guidance is
that a ladder is for getting up and down, not for working from. The institute's
position is that, as a rule, you do not work from a ladder at 2 m or above. At
2 m or more the 労働安全衛生規則 requires a work platform (作業床) wherever a
fall would put a worker at risk, and fall-protection measures where a platform
is genuinely impracticable. Insulating a duct run is work. It belongs on a
platform, not on a ladder. See [09.2.01](../ch09/09.2.01-access-rules.md).

**It does not hold up a ladder that moves.** A ladder nobody secured is one of
the five failures the institute found running through the fatal cases, and its
warning is blunt: once a ladder starts to shift, stopping it is very hard.
Textbook three-point contact on an unsecured ladder buys you a well-executed
fall. Securing, angle and footing are [09.1.04](../ch09/09.1.04-positioning.md).

**It does not decide whether you should be on a ladder at all.** Where work is
done at a height or depth of more than 1.5 m, article 526 of the
労働安全衛生規則 obliges your employer to provide equipment for getting up and
down safely, and its second paragraph obliges the worker to use that equipment
once it is provided. The employer's duty has one narrow exception, for where
providing it is markedly difficult because of the nature of the work — that
exception is your employer's to invoke and account for, never yours to assume.

So if a stair tower or a proper platform is already there, climbing a ladder
beside it with perfect technique is the wrong answer to the right question. And
if there is no safe way up at all, that is a question for your supervisor before
you start, not a gap for you to bridge.

### 脚立 is a different question

Most of your access in occupied buildings will be a stepladder, not a leaning
ladder. Do not assume this article covers it. The same leaflet gives 脚立 its own
separate rules — do not stand on the top plate, do not straddle it, do not climb
down facing outwards, do not go up or down holding objects, brace your body
against the top plate or a rung for stability — and no source consulted here
states three-point contact for stepladders in the way it is stated for
ladders. Treat 脚立 as its own topic, ask how your crew works off one,
and see [09.1.01](../ch09/09.1.01-selection.md).

### Why a short fall kills

Worth knowing here, because it changes how you treat a climb that feels too short
to bother about. The association's 2025 analysis found that in deaths from these
low falls, head injury dominates, and that the workers who died almost always
fell into one of three groups: no 保護帽 at all, a helmet rated for falling
objects rather than for falls, or a helmet that came off on the way down. Chin
strap done up, every time. See [08.1.01](../ch08/08.1.01-helmet.md).

## Worked example

Take the institute's ten-year figures and be strict with them, because a fifth of
the cases are incomplete.

103 fatal falls from ladders in construction. 52 while climbing, 29 while
working, 22 unrecorded.

52 out of 103 is 50.5 percent. But 22 unknowns is enough to move that number, so
test it instead of repeating it. Assume the worst case for the argument: every
one of the 22 unrecorded deaths was someone working from the ladder rather than
climbing it. That gives 51 working against 52 climbing.

Climbing still comes first. There is no way of distributing the missing cases
that turns climbing into a minor category.

Now the same table by trade. 設備工事 contributed 35 of the 103, and 15 of those
35 happened during 昇降. Over ten years that is 1.5 building-services workers a
year killed on the way up or down a ladder, and 3.5 a year killed by ladders
altogether.

Those are small numbers, and they are the point. The industry-association
leaflet that carries this rule has a title that translates roughly as: working
at low height — are you letting your guard down? Ladders are not usually dangerous. They are
usually completely fine, right up until the once.

**On the vintage of these figures.** The institute's study covers the ten years
from fiscal 2004 to fiscal 2013, which the document itself states. It carries no
publication date that could be found, so it is dated here by its data rather
than by its cover. The association's 2025 leaflet gives
the more recent picture: across all industries, falls from ladders or stepladders
killed somewhere between 20 and 30 people a year over the decade to 2022, with
construction about half of that. Different scope, same order of magnitude, no
sign of the problem going away.

## Common mistakes

- **Carrying material up in one hand.** The commonest version of the mistake, and
  the one the fatal-case analysis keeps finding. You are on two points for every
  step of the climb. Send it up separately.
- **Treating light material as an exception.** A coil of banding weighs almost
  nothing and still occupies a hand. The rule counts hands, not kilograms.
- **Coming down facing outwards** because it is quicker and you can see the floor.
  The institute names this for ladders; the association's leaflet names it for
  stepladders. Turn round on either.
- **Counting a forearm, an elbow or a knee as a point.** Both sources describe the
  rule in terms of hands and feet. Whether hooking an arm through the ladder
  counts as contact is not addressed by any source consulted here, so do not
  assume it does — ask your supervisor, and until you have an answer, treat it
  as not counting.
- **Reading the rule as covering standing still.** It governs 昇降, going up and
  down. What you may do once you have stopped is a different rule, and the
  answer there is mostly: not this, use a platform.
- **Applying it perfectly to a ladder nobody secured.** Three-point contact
  assumes the ladder stays where it was put. If nobody tied it and nobody is
  footing it, the technique is not the thing protecting you.
- **Skipping it because the climb is short.** The whole reason that 2025 leaflet
  exists is that low work is where people relax. A fall of well under 2 m kills
  by head injury.
- **Assuming this article settles it for your site.** It gives you the rule two
  Japanese safety bodies publish and the numbers behind it. Your site's own
  method statement and your KY meeting govern what you actually do, and if
  the two ever disagree, follow the site and ask why.

# Why insulation reduces heat flow

## What it is

Insulation does not block heat the way a wall blocks rain. Heat always moves
from hot to cold, and nothing you install will stop that. What insulation does
is **slow it down** — it makes the heat take a longer, harder path.

The trick is air. Still air is a genuinely poor conductor of heat. Every
insulation material you will handle on site — glass wool, rock wool, foam
glass, polyurethane — is mostly air by volume. The solid part exists to hold
that air in place and stop it moving.

## Why it matters on the job

This one idea explains most of the mistakes that ruin a job, so it is worth
understanding rather than memorising.

If the value comes from *still* air, then anything that lets the air move, or
replaces the air with something else, destroys the insulation. That is why:

- Compressed insulation performs worse than uncompressed. You squeezed the air
  out. Wire tied too tight, or material forced into a gap that is too small,
  loses a large part of its value.
- Wet insulation performs far worse than dry. Water conducts heat roughly
  twenty times better than air. Water in the material is not a small problem
  to be dried out later — it is an immediate failure of the thing you installed.
- Gaps matter more than they look. Air that can circulate through a gap carries
  heat across by convection, bypassing the material entirely.

When a supervisor tells you not to crush the material or not to leave it out in
the rain, this is why. It is not tidiness. It is the entire function of the
product.

## In detail

Heat moves three ways, and insulation has to defeat all three at once.
See [02.2.01](../ch02/02.2.01-conduction.md) for the modes themselves.

**Conduction (熱伝導).** Heat passes through material by molecules bumping into
their neighbours. Densely packed solids pass it easily — this is why metal feels
cold to the touch, it is pulling heat out of your hand quickly. Gases are packed
far more loosely, so conduction through them is slow. Insulation maximises the
proportion of gas along the path.

**Convection.** Air that can move carries heat with it bodily. A pocket of air
warms at the hot face, rises, and delivers its heat to the cold face. This is
why an empty air gap is a mediocre insulator but a foam full of *sealed* air
pockets is a good one — the pockets are too small for circulation to get going.

**Radiation.** Heat also crosses as infrared energy, needing no material at all.
At the temperatures you meet on most pipework this is the smallest of the three,
but it grows quickly as temperature rises, which is why high-temperature work
sometimes uses reflective foil layers.

**Thermal resistance (熱抵抗).** The useful way to think about the result is
resistance: how hard it is for heat to get through. More thickness means more
resistance. A material with lower conductivity means more resistance for the
same thickness. Layers add up — the pipe wall, the insulation, the outer
jacketing each contribute, though the insulation dominates by a wide margin.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating insulation as a barrier.** It slows heat; it never stops it. A
  correctly insulated hot pipe is still losing heat, just slowly enough to be
  acceptable. Expecting the outer surface to be at room temperature is wrong.
- **Compressing material to make it fit.** If it does not fit, the thickness or
  the size is wrong. Forcing it destroys the performance you were paid to
  deliver, and the defect is invisible once jacketing goes on.
- **Assuming wet material will dry out.** In service it usually will not, and on
  cold systems it will get worse rather than better. Wet material gets replaced,
  not dried.
- **Leaving unsealed gaps at joints.** A small continuous gap can carry more
  heat than a large area of good material, because circulating air moves heat
  far faster than conduction through the material does.
- **Thinking thicker is always proportionally better.** Doubling thickness does
  not halve the heat loss on a pipe — the geometry works against you. See
  [02.3.02](../ch02/02.3.02-effect-of-thickness.md).

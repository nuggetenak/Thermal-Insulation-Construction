# Why insulation reduces heat flow

## What it is

Insulation does not block heat the way a wall blocks rain. Heat always moves
from hot to cold, and nothing you install will stop that. What insulation does
is **slow it down** — it makes the heat take a longer, harder path.

The trick is air. Still air is a genuinely poor conductor of heat. Every
insulation material you will handle — glass wool, rock wool, cellular glass,
polyurethane foam — is mostly air by volume. The solid part exists only to hold
that air in place and stop it moving.

## Why it matters on the job

This single idea explains most of the mistakes that ruin a job, so it is worth
understanding rather than memorising.

If the value comes from *still* air, then anything that lets the air move, or
replaces the air with something else, destroys the insulation. That is why:

- Compressed insulation performs worse than uncompressed. You squeezed the air
  out. Wire tied too tight, or material forced into a gap that is too small,
  loses a large part of its value.
- Wet insulation performs far worse than dry, by a much larger margin than most
  people expect. The numbers are below.
- Gaps matter more than they look. Air that can circulate through a gap carries
  heat across bodily, bypassing the material entirely.

When a supervisor tells you not to crush the material or not to leave it out in
the rain, this is why. It is not tidiness. It is the entire function of the
product you are being paid to install.

## In detail

Heat moves three ways and insulation has to defeat all three at once. See
[02.2.01](../ch02/02.2.01-conduction.md) for the modes themselves.

**Conduction (熱伝導).** Heat passes through material by molecules bumping into
their neighbours. Densely packed solids pass it easily; gases are packed far
more loosely, so conduction through them is slow.

**Convection.** Air that can move carries heat with it. A pocket of air warms at
the hot face, rises, and delivers its heat to the cold face. This is why an
empty air gap is a mediocre insulator but a foam full of sealed pockets is a
good one — the pockets are too small for circulation to get going.

**Radiation.** Heat also crosses as infrared energy, needing no material at all.
At the temperatures you meet on most pipework this is the smallest of the three,
but it grows quickly as temperature rises.

### The number that matters

Every material has a **thermal conductivity**, written as the Greek letter
lambda and measured in watts per metre per kelvin, W/(m·K). Lower is better. You
will see this value on every product data sheet you ever handle.

| Material | lambda, W/(m·K) |
|---|---|
| Still air | 0.026 |
| Rigid polyurethane foam | from 0.022 |
| Glass wool, dry | 0.030 to 0.046 |
| Rock wool, dry | 0.032 to 0.044 |
| Cellular glass | about 0.041 |
| Water | 0.598 |
| Carbon steel | on the order of 50 |

Two things jump out of that table.

First, every insulation material sits close to the conductivity of still air.
They are not exotic substances — they are structures for holding air. The
differences between them come from pore size, density and how well the air is
sealed in, not from the solid material being special.

Second, water conducts heat about twenty-three times better than air. Water is
not a contaminant that slightly degrades performance. On the scale that matters
here, it behaves like a conductor.

### Thermal resistance

The useful way to think about the result is resistance — how hard it is for heat
to get through. For a flat layer, resistance is thickness divided by lambda,
with thickness in metres, giving a result in square-metre-kelvin per watt.

More thickness means more resistance. Lower lambda means more resistance for the
same thickness. Layers add up: the pipe wall, the insulation and the outer
jacketing each contribute, though the insulation dominates by a wide margin.
Steel at roughly 50 is about two thousand times more conductive than still air,
so the pipe wall itself contributes almost nothing to the total.

On pipework the arithmetic is less generous than on a flat wall, because each
added millimetre of thickness also increases the outer surface area that heat
escapes from. Doubling the thickness on a pipe does not halve the heat loss. See
[02.3.02](../ch02/02.3.02-effect-of-thickness.md).

## Worked example

Take a 50 mm glass wool section, dry, with a lambda of 0.0343.

Resistance is 0.050 divided by 0.0343, which is 1.46.

Now the same section after it has taken up moisture, measured at a lambda of
0.136.

Resistance is 0.050 divided by 0.136, which is 0.37.

The resistance has fallen to about a quarter. To recover the original
performance using the wet material you would need roughly 200 mm of it instead
of 50 mm — four times the thickness, on a pipe with clearance for one.

This is why wet material gets replaced rather than dried. Once it is in service
behind jacketing it does not dry, and on a cold system it gets worse rather than
better as condensation keeps forming. If material has been rained on before
installation, say so before you fit it. Fitting it and staying quiet buries a
defect nobody can see until the system underperforms or the pipe corrodes
underneath.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating insulation as a barrier.** It slows heat; it never stops it. A
  correctly insulated hot pipe is still losing heat, just slowly enough to be
  acceptable. Expecting the outer surface to reach room temperature is wrong.
- **Compressing material to make it fit.** If it does not fit, the thickness or
  the size is wrong. Forcing it destroys performance, and the defect is
  invisible once jacketing goes on.
- **Assuming wet material will dry out.** It will not, and the worked example
  shows what it costs. This is one of the few defects that is both completely
  invisible after installation and severe.
- **Leaving unsealed gaps at joints.** A small continuous gap can carry more
  heat than a large area of good material, because circulating air moves heat
  far faster than conduction through the material does.
- **Reading a lambda value off a data sheet and assuming it holds on site.**
  Published figures are measured dry, at a stated mean temperature. Conductivity
  rises with temperature and rises sharply with moisture. The data sheet is the
  best case, not the working case.
- **Thinking thicker is always proportionally better.** On a pipe the geometry
  works against you, and past a certain point extra thickness buys very little.
